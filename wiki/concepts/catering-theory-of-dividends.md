# Catering Theory of Dividends

The catering theory of dividends holds that payout decisions are driven by time-varying investor demand: managers initiate or raise dividends when investors place a stock-price premium on payers and omit or cut them when investors prefer non-payers. Its central construct is the *dividend premium* — a stock-price-based measure of the relative valuation of dividend payers versus non-payers — which serves as a proxy for prevailing, potentially sentiment-driven demand. Catering is a behavioral, demand-side theory: it treats payout as a response to fluctuating and possibly irrational investor preferences rather than to fundamentals, agency costs, or fixed tax-based clienteles. It was introduced to explain why the aggregate propensity to pay dividends swings over time — a time-series pattern that supply-side theories struggled to fit — and it has since been extended to dividend changes, to share repurchases, and internationally, while drawing sharp challenges from risk-based and lifecycle rivals. Catering must be kept distinct from the stable-preference clientele theory: it concerns *time-varying* market-wide demand and managerial response to a fluctuating premium, whereas clienteles concern structurally stable investor groups (see [[dividend-clienteles]]).

## The core proposition and the dividend premium

The foundational claim is that the decision to pay dividends is driven by prevailing investor demand for dividend payers: managers cater by paying when investors put a stock-price premium on payers and by not paying when investors prefer non-payers ([[baker-2004-a-catering-theory-of-dividends]]). To make this testable, the theory operationalizes demand through the dividend premium, a relative-valuation measure, and the original test constructs four distinct stock-price-based measures of investor demand for payers. The central empirical signature is asymmetric across the initiation and omission margins: by each of the four measures, non-payers tend to *initiate* dividends when demand is high, and by some measures payers tend to *omit* dividends when demand is low ([[baker-2004-a-catering-theory-of-dividends]]). Further analysis in that work argues these initiation and omission patterns are better explained by catering than by competing dividend theories. The construct is deliberately demand-side and behavioral: the premium reflects how the market currently prices the "dividend" attribute, not the fundamentals of any individual firm, so managers who supply dividends when the premium is high are meeting a market-wide taste rather than signaling firm-specific information. This framing is what allows catering to speak to aggregate time-series movements that firm-level supply-side stories leave unexplained.

## Explaining the propensity-to-pay trends

Catering's original empirical payoff was its account of the aggregate propensity to pay. Applying the Fama–French methodology, the theory identifies four distinct trends in the propensity to pay dividends between 1963 and 2000 and shows that each lines up with a corresponding fluctuation in catering incentives: the propensity to pay rises when the dividend-premium proxy is positive and falls when it is negative ([[baker-2004-appearing-and-disappearing-dividends-the-link-to-catering]]). The lone disconnect between premium and propensity is attributed to Nixon-era dividend controls, an administrative intervention that severed the ordinary demand–supply link. This time-series alignment is the theory's signature prediction: appearing and disappearing dividends track the sign of the premium. Catering thereby offers a demand-side reinterpretation of the Fama–French "disappearing dividends" baseline, in which the fraction of payers fell from 66.5% to 20.8% and part of the decline reflected a lower propensity to pay conditional on characteristics ([[fama-2001-disappearing-dividends-changing-firm-characteristics-or-lower-propensity]]). Where Fama and French document the lower propensity as a fact to be explained, catering supplies a candidate driver — a negative dividend premium over the disappearing period — thus positioning the two literatures as question and proposed answer rather than as rivals.

The propensity-to-pay series that catering seeks to explain has itself been refined and partly reframed, which bears on what catering must account for. A later decomposition of both the 1970s–2000 decrease and the 2000–2018 recovery finds that changing firm characteristics and changing proclivity to pay each drove about half of the disappearance, whereas a higher proclivity drove 82% of the *reappearance*, with the residual traced to reduced earnings volatility; crucially, the trends are driven by newly listed and delisted firms rather than by initiations and omissions among survivors ([[michaely-2022-disappearing-and-reappearing-dividends]]). Two features of this evidence tighten the target for catering. First, that reappearance is a proclivity story keeps a demand-side driver such as catering in play, since a rising propensity conditional on characteristics is exactly what a positive dividend premium would predict. Second, the finding that list/delist composition, not initiate/omit behavior, moves the aggregate is awkward for a theory built on the initiation and omission margins ([[baker-2004-a-catering-theory-of-dividends]]). A parallel challenge questions whether there is any conditional decline to explain at all: measured by total net cash disbursed to equity holders rather than dividends alone, the conditional propensity to distribute has *not* fallen over three decades, and net payout yields have if anything risen ([[grullon-2011-has-the-propensity-to-pay-out-declined]]). If "disappearing dividends" is partly a channel-shift from dividends to repurchases rather than a genuine withdrawal of payout, then catering's account of the dividend series must be paired with its repurchase-premium extension to describe the full payout picture. The worldwide version of the trend nonetheless survives characteristic controls — a large 33-country study finds the propensity to pay has declined even after controlling for firm characteristics and is sharper in civil-law countries ([[fatemi-2012-yes-dividends-are-disappearing-worldwide-evidence]]), leaving a conditional decline for demand-side theories to address and foreshadowing the legal-regime boundary catering runs into abroad.

## Extension to dividend changes and to repurchases

Catering was quickly extended beyond the initiate/omit decision to the intensive margin of existing dividends. The extended model shows that both the decision to change a dividend and the *magnitude* of the change depend on the premium the capital market places on dividends, and that the stock-market reaction to dividend changes itself depends on the dividend premium — the market rewards managers for considering investor demand when setting dividend levels ([[li-2006-dividend-changes-and-catering-incentives]]). The framework has also been carried over to share repurchases through a *repurchase premium*: firms' choice to repurchase responds positively to the market's time-varying repurchase premium, and catering behavior can then be used as a novel test of the dividend-substitution hypothesis, with the dividend premium negatively affecting the repurchase choice and the repurchase premium negatively affecting the dividend choice ([[jiang-2013-share-repurchases-catering-and-dividend-substitution]]). A closely related construct, the "difference premium" measuring the relative preference for repurchases over dividends, shows that firms channel a higher fraction of incremental payout toward repurchases when this premium is high and that the market reacts more favorably when firms act in accordance with the catering hypothesis ([[kulchania-2013-catering-driven-substitution-in-corporate-payouts]]). The empirical signature across these extensions is that both the size of a payout change and its announcement reaction move with the relevant premium.

## International and cross-institutional evidence

Catering's generality has been probed across legal regimes, with results that make it conditional rather than universal. An international test across twenty-three countries finds evidence of catering among firms incorporated in common-law countries but not in civil-law nations, and the effect persists even after controlling for the firm lifecycle ([[ferris-2009-catering-effects-in-corporate-dividend-policy-the-international]]). The interpretation ties catering to investor protection: where the legal regime and its protections permit, investors both force dividends from managers and place a high value on payers, while civil-law firms fail to cater, plausibly because controlling shareholders consume private benefits or are indifferent to temporary misvaluation. A separate 18-country study reaches the same common-law/civil-law split — catering incentives persist only in common-law countries — but adds a crucial qualification examined in the next section ([[kuo-2013-what-drives-the-disappearing-dividends-phenomenon]]). Broader international evidence on the determinants of dividend policy finds little systematic relation outside the U.S. between the relative prices of payers and non-payers and the propensity to pay, casting doubt on catering alongside signaling and clientele explanations while supporting agency-cost lifecycle theories ([[denis-2008-why-do-firms-pay-dividends-international-evidence-on]]).

> **Cross-paper pattern:** Two independent multi-country studies converge on a legal-regime boundary for catering: it appears among common-law firms but not civil-law firms ([[ferris-2009-catering-effects-in-corporate-dividend-policy-the-international]]; [[kuo-2013-what-drives-the-disappearing-dividends-phenomenon]]). Catering is thus not a universal law of payout but a phenomenon conditioned on investor protection — present where minority investors can express and enforce a taste for dividends, absent where they cannot.

## Micro-foundations and limits: whose demand, and can managers meet it?

If catering is a response to investor demand, a natural question is whose demand, measured how, and whether managers can actually satisfy it. One line builds a catering index from the *revealed* payout preferences of the mutual funds that hold a firm's stock, showing that catering is constrained by market segmentation and by dispersion in investor payout preferences — a firm serving many "masters" with conflicting tastes cannot cater fully — while firms that raise their catering index experience an increase in value and a more positive market reaction to equity issues and dividend payouts ([[manconi-2013-a-servant-to-many-masters-competing-shareholder-preferences]]). A more recent approach measures time-varying dividend demand directly from Internet search volume for dividend-related terms, finding that investors search more for dividends when economic conditions are poor (peaking during the COVID-19 pandemic), that firms initiate or increase dividends when this sentiment is stronger and especially in high-sentiment regions, and that shifts in sentiment predict higher demand and higher returns for high-dividend stocks ([[kumar-2022-dividend-sentiment-catering-incentives-and-return-predictability]]). These studies supply the behavioral micro-foundation the original stock-price premium left implicit ([[baker-2004-a-catering-theory-of-dividends]]): demand is heterogeneous, partly sentiment-driven, and only imperfectly addressable given segmentation, which bounds how much catering any single firm can do.

## Challenges: risk, lifecycle, and fundamentals

The most serious challenge to catering is that its proxies may be standing in for risk. Reexamining the disappearing-dividends puzzle through the lens of risk finds that risk is a significant determinant of the propensity to pay and explains roughly 40% of the decline, and — decisively for catering — that fad or catering proxies matter only absent risk controls and become insignificant once risk is controlled ([[hoberg-2009-disappearing-dividends-catering-and-risk]]). The international literature echoes this displacement: even where catering appears in common-law countries, it provides little support after adjusting for risk, so that catering incentives are read as reflecting the risk-reward relationship in the changing propensity to pay ([[kuo-2013-what-drives-the-disappearing-dividends-phenomenon]]). Cross-country evidence on dividend determinants likewise favors agency-cost lifecycle theories over catering ([[denis-2008-why-do-firms-pay-dividends-international-evidence-on]]). The empirical signature of this challenge is stark: catering proxies lose significance whenever a credible risk control is added.

> **Cross-paper pattern:** A risk-based displacement recurs across the challenger studies. Catering proxies are significant in isolation but fall to insignificance once risk is controlled ([[hoberg-2009-disappearing-dividends-catering-and-risk]]; [[kuo-2013-what-drives-the-disappearing-dividends-phenomenon]]), and international determinant studies favor lifecycle over catering explanations ([[denis-2008-why-do-firms-pay-dividends-international-evidence-on]]). The shared implication is that at least part of what the dividend premium captures is compensation for risk rather than pure sentiment.

## Current status and open questions

Catering survives as a partial, regime-conditional explanation rather than a settled account of payout. It is strongest at the initiation margin, in common-law regimes, and where active adjustment of dividend levels can be observed ([[baker-2004-a-catering-theory-of-dividends]]; [[baker-2004-appearing-and-disappearing-dividends-the-link-to-catering]]; [[li-2006-dividend-changes-and-catering-incentives]]; [[ferris-2009-catering-effects-in-corporate-dividend-policy-the-international]]), and it extends coherently to repurchases through a repurchase or difference premium ([[jiang-2013-share-repurchases-catering-and-dividend-substitution]]; [[kulchania-2013-catering-driven-substitution-in-corporate-payouts]]). It is weakest once risk is controlled and in civil-law regimes ([[hoberg-2009-disappearing-dividends-catering-and-risk]]; [[kuo-2013-what-drives-the-disappearing-dividends-phenomenon]]; [[denis-2008-why-do-firms-pay-dividends-international-evidence-on]]). The open questions concern the true source of the demand the premium proxies — sentiment, risk, or segmentation-driven preference dispersion ([[manconi-2013-a-servant-to-many-masters-competing-shareholder-preferences]]; [[kumar-2022-dividend-sentiment-catering-incentives-and-return-predictability]]) — and how to separate catering cleanly from stable clienteles and from the lifecycle.

> **Current assessment (2026-07):** Catering is best regarded as a partial and regime-conditional driver of payout. It reliably predicts initiation timing and the direction of dividend changes in common-law markets, but its explanatory power weakens sharply under risk controls and largely vanishes in civil-law regimes. The unsettled core question is whether the dividend premium proxies genuine sentiment-driven demand or is partly a repackaging of risk, and how to distinguish time-varying catering from stable tax-based clienteles.
