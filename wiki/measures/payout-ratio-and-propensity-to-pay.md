# Payout Ratio and Propensity to Pay

The payout literature rests on two workhorse dependent variables that answer different questions and must not be conflated. The **payout ratio** is an intensive-margin measure — *how much* a firm distributes, scaled by earnings, assets, or net income — while the **propensity to pay** is an extensive-margin measure — *whether* a firm pays at all, most usefully the conditional propensity estimated after controlling for firm characteristics. The distinction is not pedantic: many apparent contradictions in the collection dissolve once the measure is pinned down, because dividends "disappear" on the extensive margin (fewer payers) even as aggregate dollars concentrate and rise, and net cash returned to shareholders does not decline at all. This page documents what each measure captures, the standard conditional-logit operationalization, the covariates that scale it, the composition-versus-behavior decomposition that reads the disappearing-dividends trend, the pivotal switch from a dividends-only to a total-payout denominator, cross-sample comparability, and the biases that trap the unwary. Its job is to define the operationalizations and flag the pitfalls, not to adjudicate the substantive debates these measures feed.

## What is being measured: intensive vs. extensive margin

The two margins are logically independent. The intensive margin asks, conditional on paying, how large the distribution is relative to a base — the classic dividend payout ratio, or a broader total-payout ratio that adds repurchases to the numerator. The extensive margin asks a binary question — does the firm pay — and its aggregate counterpart is the proportion of firms that pay, which fell from 66.5% in 1978 to 20.8% in 1999 ([[fama-2001-disappearing-dividends-changing-firm-characteristics-or-lower-propensity]]). A second, orthogonal choice concerns *what* is counted: dividends only, or total payout including repurchases, or net payout after equity issuance — formally $\text{net payout} = \text{dividends} + \text{repurchases} - \text{equity issuance}$. Because repurchases became the dominant payout form and dividend-only firms became largely extinct, a dividends-only measure increasingly mismeasures the underlying distribution behavior ([[skinner-2008-the-evolving-relation-between-earnings-dividends-and-stock]]). The net-cash measure — dividends plus repurchases minus issuance — behaves altogether differently from the dividend count, which is the single most consequential measurement fact in the disappearing-dividends debate ([[grullon-2011-has-the-propensity-to-pay-out-declined]]).

## Standard operationalization of the propensity to pay

The canonical extensive-margin tool is the Fama–French conditional-logit approach: regress the pay/not-pay indicator on firm characteristics (size, profitability, growth, and the earned/contributed capital mix), then read the unexplained shift over time — the change in fitted probability holding characteristics fixed — as the change in *propensity* or *proclivity* net of composition. Schematically, the expected payer fraction is $\widehat{p}_t = \frac{1}{N_t}\sum_i \Lambda(\hat{\beta}\,x_{it})$ using base-period coefficients $\hat{\beta}$; the gap between the actual fraction and $\widehat{p}_t$ isolates the behavioral component, while the movement of $\widehat{p}_t$ itself captures the compositional component. The empirical signature that motivates the method is that the propensity falls even after characteristics are held constant, so the decline is not merely a story of the firm population changing ([[fama-2001-disappearing-dividends-changing-firm-characteristics-or-lower-propensity]]). The same conditional framework travels internationally, where the propensity to pay is higher among larger, more profitable firms and those whose retained earnings form a large fraction of equity, across the US, Canada, UK, Germany, France, and Japan ([[denis-2008-why-do-firms-pay-dividends-international-evidence-on]]). Re-examination of the US series shows the method is sensitive to how the sample composition is treated: attributing the decline instead to the changing composition of firms over time, and surfacing an IPO-cohort effect whereby the decade a firm went public shapes its initial payout policy while a within-cohort lifecycle effect persists ([[banyi-2014-declining-propensity-to-pay-a-re-examination-of]]).

## Determinants that scale the measures

The covariates in the conditional model are not nuisance controls; they define the benchmark of a "reasonable payer" against which propensity is judged. The dominant covariate is the earned/contributed capital mix — the fraction of equity that is retained earnings rather than contributed capital — which has a quantitatively greater impact on the decision to pay than profitability or growth opportunities ([[deangelo-2006-dividend-policy-and-the-earned-contributed-capital-mix]]). This is the empirical backbone of the lifecycle reading of payout: firms rich in earned equity are the reasonable candidates to pay, and it is exactly those firms whose propensity fell most, at roughly twice the overall Fama–French reduction ([[deangelo-2006-dividend-policy-and-the-earned-contributed-capital-mix]]). Profitability, size, and growth remain the standard supplementary covariates and carry the expected signs internationally, with larger and more profitable firms more likely to pay ([[denis-2008-why-do-firms-pay-dividends-international-evidence-on]]; [[fatemi-2012-yes-dividends-are-disappearing-worldwide-evidence]]). Because these determinants both explain incidence and calibrate the intensive ratio, mis-specifying them contaminates both margins at once, which is why the earned/contributed mix has become a near-mandatory control.

## Composition vs. behavior: decomposing the trend

The core inferential task is separating a changing firm population (composition) from a changing willingness to pay conditional on type (behavior/proclivity). The founding decomposition splits the 1978–1999 decline into a compositional tilt toward small, unprofitable, high-growth new listings and a genuine behavioral drop that holds regardless of characteristics, judging the latter at least as important ([[fama-2001-disappearing-dividends-changing-firm-characteristics-or-lower-propensity]]). Extending through the reappearance, a later decomposition finds characteristics and proclivity each drive about half of the 1970s–2000 disappearance, while 82% of the 2000–2018 reappearance is driven by higher proclivity among stable, profitable firms and the residual 18% by reduced earnings volatility ([[michaely-2022-disappearing-and-reappearing-dividends]]). A structurally important finding for the decomposition is that newly listed and delisted firms — not initiations and omissions by continuing firms — drive the trends, which means the measure is as much about the entry and exit of firm-years as about behavior within a fixed panel ([[michaely-2022-disappearing-and-reappearing-dividends]]). The composition reading is reinforced by evidence tying the decline to the changing mix of firms and to IPO cohorts rather than to declining proclivity within firms, with the additional wrinkle that the propensity to pay was greater than expected following the 2003 dividend tax cut ([[banyi-2014-declining-propensity-to-pay-a-re-examination-of]]). The decomposition machinery therefore carries a genuine identification burden: the same aggregate series admits both a behavioral and a compositional reading depending on how firm entry and exit, IPO vintage, and tax-regime breaks are handled, and the two decompositions in the collection reach materially different weightings on proclivity versus composition even over overlapping windows ([[fama-2001-disappearing-dividends-changing-firm-characteristics-or-lower-propensity]]; [[banyi-2014-declining-propensity-to-pay-a-re-examination-of]]). This is why the conditional propensity, not the raw payer fraction, is the object the field actually models.

> **Cross-paper pattern:** Whether the disappearance is read as behavior or composition depends heavily on sample construction and horizon — Fama–French and Michaely–Moin assign a large role to falling proclivity, while the re-examination attributes it to the changing composition of firms and IPO-cohort effects ([[fama-2001-disappearing-dividends-changing-firm-characteristics-or-lower-propensity]]; [[michaely-2022-disappearing-and-reappearing-dividends]]; [[banyi-2014-declining-propensity-to-pay-a-re-examination-of]]).

## Dividends-only vs. total net payout

Switching the numerator from dividends to net cash distributed to equity holders overturns the headline conclusion. When the measure is net payout — dividends plus repurchases net of equity issuance — there is no evidence that the conditional propensity to distribute has declined over three decades; net payout yields have actually increased conditional on characteristics ([[grullon-2011-has-the-propensity-to-pay-out-declined]]). This is consistent with the substitution of repurchases for dividends and with total payout remaining anchored to earnings even as the dividend-only series fell ([[skinner-2008-the-evolving-relation-between-earnings-dividends-and-stock]]). It also reconciles with the worldwide picture, where a dividends-only lens still shows a real decline in both the average payout ratio and the proportion of payers even after controlling for characteristics — most pronounced among smaller, less profitable, higher-growth firms and in civil-law countries — precisely because that lens excludes the repurchase channel that absorbs the difference elsewhere ([[fatemi-2012-yes-dividends-are-disappearing-worldwide-evidence]]). A recurrent aggregate fact spanning these studies is that dividend dollars are highly concentrated in a small group of the largest, most profitable firms, so firm-count measures and dollar-weighted measures can move in opposite directions ([[denis-2008-why-do-firms-pay-dividends-international-evidence-on]]; [[fatemi-2012-yes-dividends-are-disappearing-worldwide-evidence]]).

## Cross-country and cross-sample comparability

Comparability across studies hinges on aligning both the numerator definition and the legal-institutional setting. A worldwide sample of more than 17,000 firms across 33 countries documents a genuine decline in the propensity to pay after controlling for characteristics, but with the decline in mean payout ratios and payer proportions far more pronounced in civil-law countries than common-law ones — a reminder that a pooled payout ratio blends behavior with legal environment ([[fatemi-2012-yes-dividends-are-disappearing-worldwide-evidence]]). The six-country evidence sharpens the point: the same conditional-propensity apparatus yields the familiar size/profitability/earned-equity gradient everywhere, but the apparent reductions in propensity are driven largely by newly listed firms failing to initiate when expected, and dividend abandonment by existing payers is economically unimportant except in Japan ([[denis-2008-why-do-firms-pay-dividends-international-evidence-on]]). The practical upshot is that comparing a payout ratio across countries or samples without harmonizing the payout definition, the treatment of new listings, and the legal regime risks attributing to behavior what is really a difference in measurement or institutions.

## Known biases and pitfalls

Several recurring traps distort inference. The first is treating incidence as intensity — reporting a falling fraction of payers as though total distributions fell, when aggregate and net-payout dollars rose ([[grullon-2011-has-the-propensity-to-pay-out-declined]]; [[fama-2001-disappearing-dividends-changing-firm-characteristics-or-lower-propensity]]). The second is an aggregation trap: because dividends concentrate in a few large firms, an equal-weighted firm-count measure and a dollar-weighted measure can diverge, so the choice of weighting is itself a modeling decision ([[denis-2008-why-do-firms-pay-dividends-international-evidence-on]]). The third is survivorship and turnover, since listing and delisting of firm-years — not within-firm decisions — drive much of the observed trend, so a measure computed on a survivor panel misstates the population dynamics ([[michaely-2022-disappearing-and-reappearing-dividends]]). The fourth is the choice of scaling denominator (earnings, assets, or price) and of numerator (dividends, total payout, or net payout), which can flip the sign of the trend ([[grullon-2011-has-the-propensity-to-pay-out-declined]]; [[skinner-2008-the-evolving-relation-between-earnings-dividends-and-stock]]). A fifth trap is treating the conditional propensity as invariant to sample construction, when in fact the estimated proclivity depends on how new listings and IPO cohorts are handled: a decline that looks behavioral in one specification reads as compositional once the entry decade is modeled ([[banyi-2014-declining-propensity-to-pay-a-re-examination-of]]; [[michaely-2022-disappearing-and-reappearing-dividends]]). A sixth, cross-country, trap is pooling civil- and common-law firms into a single ratio, since the payout-ratio decline is far sharper under civil law and pooling attributes to behavior what is partly institutional ([[fatemi-2012-yes-dividends-are-disappearing-worldwide-evidence]]).

> **Current assessment (2026-07):** For the *incidence* question the conditional propensity to pay dividends (Fama–French logit) remains canonical, but for the *substance* question — has the firm reduced what it returns to shareholders — the field now treats the conditional net-payout measure as the appropriate variable, since it is the one that does not spuriously register the dividend-to-repurchase form shift as a decline.
