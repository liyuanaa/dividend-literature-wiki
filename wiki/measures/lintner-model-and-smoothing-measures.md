# Lintner Model and Smoothing Measures

The Lintner model is the canonical partial-adjustment specification used to describe and quantify how firms set dividends, and it anchors the family of smoothing measures derived from it. Lintner (1956) posited that a firm forms a target dividend as a payout ratio applied to earnings and then closes only a fraction of the gap between that target and last period's dividend each year; the fraction closed is the speed of adjustment, and its complement is the standard scalar index of smoothing, so a low speed of adjustment means stickier, more heavily smoothed dividends. This apparatus matters because almost every empirical claim about smoothing is mediated by it: the estimated speed of adjustment is the number researchers regress on firm and country characteristics, extend from dividends to total payout, or blame for confounding signaling and predictability tests. This page documents what the model measures, its estimating equation, how the speed of adjustment and related indices are computed and validated, the main variants — total-payout Lintner models, structural estimation, and dynamic-model measures — and the known biases that complicate comparison across studies. The substantive economics of *why* firms smooth is treated on the companion concept page [[dividend-smoothing]]; this page is the "how measured" counterpart.

## What is being measured

The object of measurement is the responsiveness of dividends to earnings: a smoothing firm adjusts its dividend slowly and keeps the current dividend relatively insensitive to current earnings, whereas a non-smoothing firm passes earnings through more fully. Operationally, smoothing is captured as slow adjustment toward a target payout — a strong loading on the lagged dividend and a weak loading on current earnings ([[aivazian-2006-dividend-smoothing-and-debt-ratings]]; [[leary-2011-determinants-of-dividend-smoothing-empirical-evidence]]). The measure presupposes a well-defined target payout ratio, and a first-order choice is what payout it applies to. When the target is defined over regular dividends alone, the measure describes dividend stickiness; when it is defined over total payout (dividends plus net repurchases), it describes the smoothness of the whole distribution stream, and the two need not coincide once repurchases and special dividends are available ([[andres-2015-the-lintner-model-revisited-dividends-versus-total-payouts]]; [[lambrecht-2012-a-lintner-model-of-payout-and-managerial-rents]]). Because the quantity being estimated is a behavioral parameter rather than an accounting ratio, the same firm can look smoothing or residual depending on which payout definition and which sample the researcher adopts, which is why the measure's construction is inseparable from the substantive conclusions drawn from it.

## Formal model

Lintner's specification begins with a target dividend proportional to current earnings,

$$D_t^{*} = r\,E_t,$$

where $E_t$ is earnings in period $t$, $D_t^{*}$ is the target dividend, and $r$ is the **target payout ratio**. Firms do not jump to the target; they close only a fraction of the gap between the target and the previous dividend:

$$\Delta D_t = \alpha + c\,\bigl(D_t^{*} - D_{t-1}\bigr) + \varepsilon_t,$$

where $\Delta D_t = D_t - D_{t-1}$ is the dividend change, $\alpha$ is a drift term capturing managers' reluctance to cut, $c \in (0,1]$ is the **speed of adjustment** (SOA), and $\varepsilon_t$ is a disturbance. The scalar **smoothing index** is the complement of the speed of adjustment, $(1-c)$: a value near one denotes heavy smoothing, a value near zero a near-residual policy. Substituting the target gives the estimating reduced form in the lagged dividend and current earnings,

$$D_t = \alpha + (1-c)\,D_{t-1} + c\,r\,E_t + \varepsilon_t,$$

so a regression of the current dividend on its own lag and current earnings recovers $(1-c)$ as the coefficient on $D_{t-1}$ and $c\,r$ as the coefficient on $E_t$; the target ratio is backed out as $r = (\text{earnings coefficient})/c$. A smoothing firm shows a large lagged-dividend coefficient and a small earnings coefficient. This target-adjustment form is not merely descriptive: [[lambrecht-2012-a-lintner-model-of-payout-and-managerial-rents]] microfound it for total payout in a dynamic agency model, deriving the result that payout absorbs transitory income shocks and adjusts only gradually to permanent-income changes — exactly the partial-adjustment behavior Lintner assumed — with smoothing achieved through borrowing and lending. Estimating the same partial-adjustment equation for both dividends and total payout is the empirical strategy of [[andres-2015-the-lintner-model-revisited-dividends-versus-total-payouts]].

## Estimation and validation

In practice the reduced form is estimated firm-by-firm or in panels, and the recovered speed of adjustment is treated as the firm's smoothing measure. The measure is validated by its ability to sort firms into recognizable behavioral types: a "Lintner-style" firm exhibits a strong lagged-dividend loading and a weak current-earnings loading, while a residual-policy firm shows the reverse, flowing earnings straight through. The cleanest such validation comes from [[aivazian-2006-dividend-smoothing-and-debt-ratings]], who show that firms with bond ratings follow the traditional Lintner pattern — prior dividend very influential, current dividend relatively insensitive to current earnings — whereas firms without bond ratings pass earnings through and display little smoothing, a near-residual policy. The measure also varies coherently with firm characteristics in the U.S. cross-section, smoothing more for mature cash cows and less for young, volatile, low-yield firms ([[leary-2011-determinants-of-dividend-smoothing-empirical-evidence]]), and it travels internationally, with the speed of adjustment estimated across more than two thousand firms in twenty-four countries and related to legal protection, culture, and tax regime ([[javakhadze-2014-an-international-analysis-of-dividend-smoothing]]). Explicit Lintner-model tests also detect cross-market differences, showing that Hong Kong firms adjust faster — smooth less — than U.S. firms ([[chemmanur-2010-is-dividend-smoothing-universal-new-insights-from-a]]). The empirical signature to look for is thus a diagnostic loading pattern rather than a single threshold: the lagged-dividend-versus-earnings coefficient split is what distinguishes smoothers from residual payers.

> **Cross-paper pattern:** A recurring split validates the measure across settings — bond-rated, large, mature firms load on the lagged dividend and smooth heavily, while unrated, smaller, or emerging-market firms flow earnings through in a near-residual policy ([[aivazian-2006-dividend-smoothing-and-debt-ratings]]; [[leary-2011-determinants-of-dividend-smoothing-empirical-evidence]]; [[chemmanur-2010-is-dividend-smoothing-universal-new-insights-from-a]]; [[javakhadze-2014-an-international-analysis-of-dividend-smoothing]]). That the same estimated parameter separates these groups consistently is the main evidence that the speed of adjustment measures something real.

## Variants: dividends versus total payout

The most consequential variant extends the partial-adjustment model from regular dividends to total payout, motivated by the rise of repurchases. [[andres-2015-the-lintner-model-revisited-dividends-versus-total-payouts]] estimate Lintner partial-adjustment models for both dividends and total payouts around the 1998 introduction of repurchases in Germany, and additionally decompose the response into permanent and transitory earnings components. Their results reject the hypothesis that dividends and repurchases are perfect substitutes and find that repurchases have not taken over the role of special dividends; instead they support the flexibility hypothesis, under which regular dividends disburse permanent earnings while more flexible channels — special dividends and repurchases — disburse transitory earnings. The theoretical counterpart is [[lambrecht-2012-a-lintner-model-of-payout-and-managerial-rents]], whose model delivers total payout following the Lintner target-adjustment form and adjusting gradually to permanent income while smoothing out transitory shocks, providing the permanent/transitory logic that the empirical decomposition tests. The empirical signature that separates the two views is whether a transitory earnings shock passes into regular dividends (perfect-substitution/single-stream view) or is routed to repurchases and specials while the regular dividend holds (flexibility view). Only two papers in this corpus estimate the total-payout extension directly, so this variant rests on a thinner base than the firm-type validation above.

> **Current assessment (2026-07):** In the repurchase era the dividend-only speed of adjustment measures the stickiness of one channel, not the smoothness of total distributions, and the evidence that dividends and repurchases are not perfect substitutes ([[andres-2015-the-lintner-model-revisited-dividends-versus-total-payouts]]) means a dividend-only measure and a total-payout measure can diverge. Because the theory that motivates the whole apparatus concerns total payout ([[lambrecht-2012-a-lintner-model-of-payout-and-managerial-rents]]), the total-payout speed of adjustment is arguably the more theory-consistent object, but the dividend-only measure remains the one most comparable to the historical literature. No consensus has settled which is the "right" measure.

## Variants: structural and model-based measures

A second family of variants abandons the single reduced-form coefficient in favor of structural or dynamic-model estimation that decomposes observed smoothness into its behavioral sources. [[wu-2018-what-s-behind-smooth-dividends-evidence-from-structural]] estimates a dynamic agency model in which dividends signal earnings persistence, and uses the fitted parameters to partition measured smoothness into a shareholder-preference component and a managerial-career-concern component — an exercise a reduced-form speed of adjustment cannot perform, since that single number pools all sources. A related model-based approach treats firm value as depending on payout smoothness within a dynamic partial-equilibrium framework, yielding smoothness as an equilibrium object rather than a regression residual ([[karpavicius-2014-dividends-relevance-rigidity-and-signaling]]). Alternative scalar metrics that sidestep the target-adjustment regression entirely include volatility ratios of dividends to earnings and, for asset-pricing purposes, smoothing-robust series such as net payout and earnings ([[chen-2012-dividend-smoothing-and-predictability]]). The empirical signature these variants add is a decomposition: rather than reporting how sticky dividends are, they report how much of the stickiness is attributable to a named economic force, which is what lets the smoothing literature move from measuring the phenomenon to adjudicating its causes.

## Known biases and comparability

Estimated smoothing is not a clean measurement, and several biases limit cross-study comparison. First, repurchases contaminate dividend-only measures: because dividends and repurchases are not perfect substitutes, a dividend-only speed of adjustment can overstate the smoothness of total distributions ([[andres-2015-the-lintner-model-revisited-dividends-versus-total-payouts]]). Second, smoothing induces a spurious relation between dividends and future performance — [[karpavicius-2014-dividends-relevance-rigidity-and-signaling]] show that the empirically observed positive dividend–future-performance link is a statistical artifact of smoothing, so signaling tests built on that correlation may be misspecified. Third, institutional timing inflates measured persistence: as nearly 60% of dividend increases come in fixed four-quarter cycles, dividend policy becomes mechanically more persistent, raising apparent smoothing for reasons unrelated to any deliberate target-adjustment choice ([[andres-2017-do-what-you-did-four-quarters-ago-trends]]). Fourth, smoothing biases asset-pricing inference by burying dividend-growth predictability, so the postwar failure to predict dividend growth reflects heavy smoothing rather than absent cash-flow news, and smoothing-robust measures recover a leading role for cash-flow news ([[chen-2012-dividend-smoothing-and-predictability]]); the cross-country evidence confirms that predictability is weaker precisely where dividends are smoothed more ([[rangvid-2014-dividend-predictability-around-the-world]]). Finally, cross-country and cross-firm comparability is limited because firm characteristics, tax regimes, and institutions that shape the estimated parameter differ systematically across samples ([[javakhadze-2014-an-international-analysis-of-dividend-smoothing]]; [[chemmanur-2010-is-dividend-smoothing-universal-new-insights-from-a]]).

> **Cross-paper pattern:** Measured smoothing contaminates the very inferences it is used to draw. It manufactures a dividend–future-performance correlation that corrupts signaling tests ([[karpavicius-2014-dividends-relevance-rigidity-and-signaling]]) and it suppresses dividend-growth predictability in ways that corrupt cash-flow-versus-discount-rate inferences ([[chen-2012-dividend-smoothing-and-predictability]]; [[rangvid-2014-dividend-predictability-around-the-world]]). Any test that conditions on dividends must therefore treat the smoothing parameter as endogenous rather than as a clean regressor.

## Open questions

Three measurement questions remain unresolved. The first is whether the dividend-only or the total-payout speed of adjustment is the correct object in the repurchase era, given that the two diverge once dividends and repurchases are imperfect substitutes ([[andres-2015-the-lintner-model-revisited-dividends-versus-total-payouts]]; [[lambrecht-2012-a-lintner-model-of-payout-and-managerial-rents]]). The second is how to separate deliberate smoothing from mechanical persistence, since fixed four-quarter announcement cycles inflate the estimated parameter without reflecting any target-adjustment decision ([[andres-2017-do-what-you-did-four-quarters-ago-trends]]) and structural decomposition is needed to isolate the behavioral component ([[wu-2018-what-s-behind-smooth-dividends-evidence-from-structural]]). The third is harmonizing measures across countries and datasets so that a speed of adjustment estimated in one institutional setting is comparable to another, given the firm-level and country-level heterogeneity that drives the parameter ([[javakhadze-2014-an-international-analysis-of-dividend-smoothing]]). These measurement choices feed directly into the substantive debates surveyed on [[dividend-smoothing]].
