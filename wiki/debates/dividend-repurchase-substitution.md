# The Dividend–Repurchase Substitution Debate

The dividend–repurchase substitution debate concerns whether open-market share repurchases and cash dividends are interchangeable payout channels that managers trade off against one another, or whether they are distinct instruments serving different economic functions and different kinds of cash flow. The question became central after the explosive growth of repurchases following the early 1980s and the simultaneous decline in the fraction of firms paying dividends, two stylized facts that together invite the inference that one channel displaced the other. Three rival positions organize the literature: a *strong-substitution* view, in which repurchases are financed with funds that would otherwise have raised dividends; an *imperfect-substitute* view, in which dividends distribute permanent cash-flow shocks and repurchases distribute transient ones, so the two coexist by design; and a *complementarity* view, in which sticky dividends persist and even grow while repurchases flex around them. The stakes are conceptual as much as empirical: if repurchases substitute for dividends, the correct object of study is total "payout policy" rather than "dividend policy," and much of the disappearing-dividends phenomenon becomes an artifact of channel choice rather than a genuine retreat from disbursing cash.

## The empirical backdrop: rising repurchases and disappearing dividends

The debate must explain two contemporaneous trends. The first is the sharp fall in the proportion of publicly traded firms paying cash dividends, from 66.5% in 1978 to 20.8% in 1999 ([[fama-2001-disappearing-dividends-changing-firm-characteristics-or-lower-propensity]]), driven partly by a changing population of newly listed small, unprofitable, high-growth firms and partly by a genuine decline in the propensity to pay conditional on characteristics. The second is the rise of open-market repurchases into a first-order payout instrument ([[jagannathan-2000-financial-flexibility-and-the-choice-between-dividends-and]]; [[grullon-2002-dividends-share-repurchases-and-the-substitution-hypothesis]]), which by the mid-2000s had become the dominant form of payout for U.S. industrials ([[skinner-2008-the-evolving-relation-between-earnings-dividends-and-stock]]). *Across this literature the empirical signature is consistent: the fraction of dividend payers falls even as aggregate real payout does not, and payout concentrates among large, profitable firms.* The apparent tension between falling payer counts and undiminished aggregate payout is precisely what a substitution story is invoked to resolve, and it is why the raw incidence of dividend payment is a treacherous measure of how much cash firms actually disburse.

## The strong-substitution position

The canonical statement of strong substitution holds that firms finance their share repurchases with funds that otherwise would have been used to increase dividends, so that the rise of repurchases came partly at the expense of dividends ([[grullon-2002-dividends-share-repurchases-and-the-substitution-hypothesis]]). The evidence assembled for this view is behavioral and regulatory: young firms display a higher propensity to disburse cash through repurchases than in the past, repurchases have become the preferred vehicle for *initiating* a payout, and even large, established firms that have not cut dividends show a rising propensity to add repurchases ([[grullon-2002-dividends-share-repurchases-and-the-substitution-hypothesis]]). A structural claim accompanies the behavioral one: before 1983, regulatory constraints inhibited firms from aggressively repurchasing shares, so the post-1982 surge partly reflects the release of previously suppressed demand for the repurchase channel. Complementary time-series evidence documents that dividend-only firms have become "largely extinct," with repurchases increasingly used in place of dividends even among firms that continue to pay them ([[skinner-2008-the-evolving-relation-between-earnings-dividends-and-stock]]). Survey evidence sharpens the mechanism at the managerial level: executives regard repurchases as the more flexible instrument and often prefer them ([[brav-2005-payout-policy-in-the-21st-century]]), and catering-based tests find that a high repurchase premium negatively affects the choice to pay dividends ([[jiang-2013-share-repurchases-catering-and-dividend-substitution]]). The testable prediction is that the propensity to initiate payout through repurchases rises over time relative to dividend initiation.

## The imperfect-substitute and cash-flow-permanence position

A second camp accepts that the two channels are related but denies that they are freely interchangeable, arguing instead that they distribute different kinds of cash flow. The core hypothesis is that firms use dividend increases to distribute relatively permanent cash-flow shocks and repurchases to distribute more transient shocks ([[guay-2000-the-cash-flow-permanence-and-information-content-of]]); consistent with this, the post-shock cash flows of dividend-increasing firms revert less toward pre-shock levels than those of repurchasing firms, and the stock-price reaction to dividend increases is more positive than to repurchases even after controlling for payout size and the market's expectation about permanence. A parallel flexibility hypothesis holds that dividends are paid by firms with higher permanent operating cash flows while repurchases are used by firms with higher temporary, non-operating cash flows, that repurchasing firms have more volatile cash flows and distributions, and that repurchases are strongly pro-cyclical while dividends increase steadily; firms repurchase after poor stock-market performance and raise dividends after good performance ([[jagannathan-2000-financial-flexibility-and-the-choice-between-dividends-and]]). Time-series vector-autoregression evidence reaches the same verdict from a different method, finding that repurchases are associated with the temporary component of earnings while dividends are not, and concluding explicitly that the two are *imperfect substitutes* ([[lee-2007-time-series-behavior-of-share-repurchases-and-dividends]]).

> **Cross-paper pattern:** Three independent research designs — the reversion-and-announcement study of [[guay-2000-the-cash-flow-permanence-and-information-content-of]], the firm-type and pro-cyclicality evidence of [[jagannathan-2000-financial-flexibility-and-the-choice-between-dividends-and]], and the VAR of [[lee-2007-time-series-behavior-of-share-repurchases-and-dividends]] — converge on a single regularity: dividends track the permanent component of cash flow and repurchases track the transient component. This permanent-versus-transient sorting is the strongest micro-level evidence that the channels are imperfect rather than perfect substitutes.

## Complementarity and the resilience of dividends

A third position emphasizes that dividends have not merely survived the rise of repurchases but grown alongside them, which is difficult to reconcile with strong substitution. For U.S. industrials, dividends grow strongly after 2002 as the declining propensity to pay reverses, while banks maintain a higher and more stable propensity to pay and resist cutting dividends as the 2007–2008 financial crisis begins; repurchases, by contrast, had pushed payouts to historic levels before the crisis and then collapsed ([[floyd-2015-payout-policy-through-the-financial-crisis-the-growth]]). The empirical signature of complementarity is thus the divergent cyclical behavior of the two channels during a downturn: dividends prove sticky and defensible while repurchases are the margin that absorbs the shock. Survey evidence reinforces the asymmetry, with managers treating the maintenance of the dividend level as on par with investment decisions and funding repurchases out of residual cash flow after investment ([[brav-2005-payout-policy-in-the-21st-century]]). The decomposition of the recovery in dividend payers after 2000 likewise shows dividends reappearing rather than being permanently supplanted ([[michaely-2022-disappearing-and-reappearing-dividends]]). Together these findings caution that the channels are complements in the sense that most large payers do both, using dividends as a permanent floor and repurchases as a flexible overlay, rather than one draining the other.

## Where the positions actually disagree: total payout versus dividend counts

Much of the apparent conflict dissolves once the object of measurement shifts from the *incidence* of dividend payment to *total payout*. The decisive reconciliation is that the magnitude and duration of the disappearance in total payout are substantially smaller than the disappearance in dividends, which is read as evidence of *some* substitution between dividends and repurchases rather than a wholesale retreat from disbursing cash ([[michaely-2022-disappearing-and-reappearing-dividends]]). On this reading, the dramatic Fama–French decline in payer counts ([[fama-2001-disappearing-dividends-changing-firm-characteristics-or-lower-propensity]]) overstates the retreat from payout because it ignores the repurchase channel that partly replaced it ([[grullon-2002-dividends-share-repurchases-and-the-substitution-hypothesis]]; [[lee-2007-time-series-behavior-of-share-repurchases-and-dividends]]). The disagreement between the camps is therefore less about whether substitution occurs than about its magnitude and margin: strong-substitution proponents locate it at the initiation margin and in aggregate dollar flows, while imperfect-substitute proponents insist the residual gap — the part of the dividend decline that total-payout measures do not close — reflects the permanent-cash-flow role that repurchases cannot fill. The precise testable statement is that *total* payout should disappear far less, and recover far sooner, than dividend incidence.

## Drivers of channel choice

If the channels are imperfect substitutes, what tips a given firm toward one or the other becomes the operative question. Financial flexibility and reversibility are the most consistently invoked driver: because a repurchase program carries no implicit commitment to continue, firms facing volatile or temporary cash flows favor it ([[jagannathan-2000-financial-flexibility-and-the-choice-between-dividends-and]]; [[brav-2005-payout-policy-in-the-21st-century]]). Managerial motives specific to repurchases — attempts to time the equity market and to increase earnings per share — further pull firms toward the repurchase channel ([[brav-2005-payout-policy-in-the-21st-century]]). Time-varying investor demand supplies another lever: firms cater to a repurchase premium when investors value repurchasers and to a dividend premium when investors value payers, with each premium negatively affecting the choice of the other channel ([[jiang-2013-share-repurchases-catering-and-dividend-substitution]]). Product-market and risk shocks matter too: firms facing greater import competition face more uncertainty about future performance, are less likely to pay dividends, and substitute share repurchases for cash dividends through a risk channel ([[zhou-2013-import-competition-and-disappearing-dividends]]). Underlying all of these is the earnings level itself, which fundamentally determines the overall volume of repurchases even when other factors govern their timing ([[skinner-2008-the-evolving-relation-between-earnings-dividends-and-stock]]).

## Heterogeneity: who substitutes and who does not

Substitution is not uniform across firms, and recognizing its heterogeneity resolves several apparent contradictions in the aggregate evidence. Large, established payers rarely cut dividends but readily add repurchases on top of them, so for these firms the channels are complements at the margin even as repurchases grow ([[grullon-2002-dividends-share-repurchases-and-the-substitution-hypothesis]]; [[skinner-2008-the-evolving-relation-between-earnings-dividends-and-stock]]). Young and newly listed firms, by contrast, increasingly initiate payout through repurchases rather than dividends, so substitution is concentrated at the *initiation* margin and among newer entrants ([[grullon-2002-dividends-share-repurchases-and-the-substitution-hypothesis]]; [[fama-2001-disappearing-dividends-changing-firm-characteristics-or-lower-propensity]]). Industry matters as well: banks maintain and defend dividends through the crisis, using them to signal financial strength, whereas industrial payouts are better explained by the agency costs of free cash flow, and it is the industrials that lean most heavily on the flexible repurchase channel ([[floyd-2015-payout-policy-through-the-financial-crisis-the-growth]]).

> **Cross-paper pattern:** The evidence splits cleanly along the initiation-versus-established-payer margin. Firms initiating payout increasingly choose repurchases ([[grullon-2002-dividends-share-repurchases-and-the-substitution-hypothesis]]; [[skinner-2008-the-evolving-relation-between-earnings-dividends-and-stock]]), which drives most of the measured substitution, while established payers add repurchases without abandoning dividends ([[floyd-2015-payout-policy-through-the-financial-crisis-the-growth]]). Aggregate "substitution" is therefore largely a compositional shift in how new payers enter, not a wave of existing payers converting dividends into buybacks.

## Current state of the debate and open questions

The field has converged on "imperfect substitutes" as the modal position: the two channels are related and traded off at the margin, but they are not freely interchangeable because they distribute different cash-flow types, carry different commitment intensities, and appeal to different firms. The live disagreements concern magnitude and mechanism rather than existence. How much of the disappearing-dividends phenomenon is substitution versus genuine non-payment remains contested, though total-payout measures suggest the substitution share is real but partial ([[michaely-2022-disappearing-and-reappearing-dividends]]). The relative weights of tax incentives, financial flexibility, catering, and product-market risk in driving channel choice are not settled ([[brav-2005-payout-policy-in-the-21st-century]]; [[jiang-2013-share-repurchases-catering-and-dividend-substitution]]; [[zhou-2013-import-competition-and-disappearing-dividends]]), and the international generality of U.S. findings is largely untested in this corpus.

> **Current assessment (2026-07):** The weight of evidence favors imperfect substitution. Dividends and repurchases coexist because they distribute permanent versus transient cash flows and carry different commitment costs; substitution is concentrated at the payout-initiation margin and in aggregate dollar flows, is muted among established dividend payers, and shows up most clearly when total payout rather than dividend incidence is measured. The unresolved questions are the quantitative magnitude of substitution and the relative weight of taxes versus flexibility versus catering in channel choice.
