# Substitute vs. Outcome Model of Dividends

The substitute-versus-outcome debate concerns the causal relationship between the legal protection of minority shareholders and corporate dividend payouts, framed as two rival agency models of why firms distribute cash. Both models begin from the same premise — that a dividend is an agency cost paid to align the interests of insiders and outside investors — yet they reach diametrically opposed conclusions. In the outcome model, dividends are an *outcome* of strong shareholder rights: empowered minority investors use their legal leverage to force insiders to disgorge cash, so stronger protection predicts higher payouts. In the substitute model, dividends are a *substitute* for weak protection: insiders who anticipate returning to capital markets pay dividends voluntarily to build a reputation for decent treatment, so stronger protection predicts lower payouts. Because the two models generate opposite-signed predictions about the same protection–payout correlation, the axis is a genuine debate rather than a pair of complementary claims, and it has organised the entire law-and-finance strand of the payout literature. This page treats the debate framing itself — the two models and their conflicting predictions; the empirical *relationship* is adjudicated in [[investor-protection-and-payout]] and the underlying institutional construct is defined in [[investor-protection]].

## The question

Both models take as given that separating ownership from control creates an agency problem: insiders control corporate cash and may divert it to private benefits rather than return it to dispersed outside investors. A dividend, in this framing, is a device that transfers cash out of insider control, and the question is what makes insiders part with it. The founding treatment, La Porta, Lopez-de-Silanes, Shleifer and Vishny's cross-country study, poses the question in explicitly institutional terms and answers it against a 33-country cross-section, making legal protection of minority shareholders the variable whose variation identifies the mechanism ([[laporta-2000-agency-problems-and-dividend-policies-around-the-world]]). This design is inseparable from the broader legal-origins research program, in which cross-country differences in ownership concentration, capital-market depth, and payout all trace back to how well law shields investors from expropriation by managers and controlling shareholders ([[laporta-2000-investor-protection-and-corporate-governance]]). The distinctive difficulty is that the shared agency premise does not pin down the sign: the same logic of dividends-as-agency-cost supports either that protection *causes* payouts (by empowering the demanders) or that weak protection *induces* them (by making voluntary bonding worthwhile). A laboratory experiment reframes the question as a clean causal one — what does exogenously strengthening protection do to payouts, expropriation, and investment simultaneously — precisely because archival cross-sections cannot isolate the direction ([[lariviere-2018-shareholder-protection-and-agency-costs-an-experimental-analysis]]).

## The outcome model

The outcome model reads dividends as the product of shareholder power. Where minority investors enjoy strong legal rights — the ability to vote out directors, sue for oppression, or block self-dealing — they can pressure insiders to pay out free cash rather than let it fund empire-building or be diverted. The prediction is a positive protection–payout relationship: stronger rights, higher payouts. The founding cross-section of roughly 4,000 firms across 33 countries supports precisely this model, finding that firms in better-protected legal environments pay more ([[laporta-2000-agency-problems-and-dividend-policies-around-the-world]]). The empirical signature is not merely a level correlation but a *change* prediction: payouts should rise when governance strengthens, and the rise should concentrate in firms that were previously most vulnerable to insider entrenchment. Board reforms enacted across 40 countries provide the cleanest test — a difference-in-differences design finds firms pay higher dividends after reform, with the increase most pronounced for firms with weak pre-reform board governance and in countries with strong external governance ([[bae-2021-board-reforms-and-dividend-policy-international-evidence]]). At the micro level, minority shareholders' voting opposition to dividend proposals on the Shanghai exchange predicts significantly higher payouts the following year, and reduces expropriation by major shareholders, supplying a granular mechanism for the empowered-demander channel ([[lin-2023-minority-shareholder-voting-and-dividend-policy]]).

> **Cross-paper pattern:** The LLSV cross-section and the 40-country board-reform difference-in-differences converge on the outcome prediction from two different research designs — one exploiting cross-country institutional variation, the other a within-country regulatory shock — with the effect concentrated in exactly the weakly-governed firms the model predicts ([[laporta-2000-agency-problems-and-dividend-policies-around-the-world]]; [[bae-2021-board-reforms-and-dividend-policy-international-evidence]]; [[lin-2023-minority-shareholder-voting-and-dividend-policy]]).

A boundary case complicates the naive reading. Among Delaware master limited partnerships, *weaker* governance is associated with *higher* cash payouts that destroy firm value — consistent not with empowered minorities extracting cash but with the general partner tunneling resources out at the expense of limited unitholders ([[atanassov-2018-corporate-governance-and-dividend-policy-evidence-of-tunneling]]). High payouts, in other words, are not always a sign that the outcome mechanism is working; the same observable can mask extraction.

## The substitute model

The substitute model inverts the causal arrow. Where legal protection is weak, insiders who intend to raise external equity in the future have a private incentive to establish a reputation for treating minority shareholders decently, and a costly, visible, repeated commitment — paying large and stable dividends — is the bonding device that builds it. The prediction is a negative protection–payout relationship: the weaker the law, the greater the need to substitute a voluntary reputational commitment, and the higher the payout. The founding paper names this model and states its opposite-signed prediction explicitly, even though its own cross-section does not favour it ([[laporta-2000-agency-problems-and-dividend-policies-around-the-world]]). Shock-based studies supply the model's strongest empirical support. Firms in countries with weak insider-trading laws exhibit a higher propensity to pay, larger dividend amounts, and greater dividend smoothing, and the market values those dividends more highly — a pattern the authors interpret as dividends serving as a substitute bonding mechanism where legal protection fails ([[brockman-2014-the-impact-of-insider-trading-laws-on-dividend]]). The empirical signature is therefore multidimensional: not just higher payout levels but greater smoothing and higher dividend valuation where protection is weakest.

Two further regulatory-shock studies reinforce the substitute logic by showing payouts *fall* when protection or its functional equivalents strengthen. The staggered initiation of country-level M&A laws — which raises the threat of takeover and thereby the market's disciplinary pressure on managers — significantly reduces both the likelihood and the amount of dividends across 34 countries, because a credible takeover market lowers the need to signal commitment through costly payouts ([[glendening-2016-the-market-for-corporate-control-and-dividend-policies]]). Similarly, mandatory IFRS adoption and initial insider-trading-law enforcement, both of which improve the public information environment, make firms less likely to pay or increase dividends and more likely to cut them, as better information reduces managers' need to bond via payout ([[hail-2014-dividend-payouts-and-information-shocks]]).

## Where they actually disagree

The disagreement is sharp and can be stated as a sign flip on a single coefficient. Let $\rho$ denote the correlation between the strength of minority-shareholder protection and dividend payout. The outcome model predicts $\rho > 0$; the substitute model predicts $\rho < 0$. The models differ on three linked dimensions. First, on **sign**: whether stronger rights raise or lower payouts. Second, on the **direction of causation**: the outcome model runs protection → minority leverage → forced disgorgement, whereas the substitute model runs weak protection → insiders' anticipated financing need → voluntary payout. Third, on the **binding motive**: the outcome model makes extraction-prevention by empowered outsiders the operative force, the substitute model makes reputation-building by self-interested insiders the operative force. What the two share is the treatment of dividends as an agency cost that aligns insider and outsider interests — a point the experimental treatment makes explicit, noting that under either model the payout is a cost incurred to bridge the insider-investor divide ([[lariviere-2018-shareholder-protection-and-agency-costs-an-experimental-analysis]]; [[laporta-2000-agency-problems-and-dividend-policies-around-the-world]]). Because they agree on the premise and disagree only on the sign, no single correlation can confirm one without disconfirming the other — which is why the reconciliation turns on scope conditions rather than a flat verdict ([[chae-2009-how-corporate-governance-affects-payout-policy-under-agency]]).

## Empirical anchors

The decisive evidence spans three research designs pulling in partly different directions. The founding 33-country cross-section is the anchor for the outcome model: across roughly 4,000 firms, better minority protection is associated with higher payouts ([[laporta-2000-agency-problems-and-dividend-policies-around-the-world]]). The laboratory experiment is the anchor for causal identification, and its result is subtle: strengthening investor protection raises dividend payout ratios about fivefold, consistent with the outcome model, but it *also* raises insider expropriation roughly twofold and reduces outsider investment by about 45%, revealing agency costs that neither archival model had isolated ([[lariviere-2018-shareholder-protection-and-agency-costs-an-experimental-analysis]]). The shock-based studies are the anchors for the substitute model and for conditionality: board reforms raise payouts ([[bae-2021-board-reforms-and-dividend-policy-international-evidence]]), while M&A-law and information shocks lower them ([[glendening-2016-the-market-for-corporate-control-and-dividend-policies]]; [[brockman-2014-the-impact-of-insider-trading-laws-on-dividend]]; [[hail-2014-dividend-payouts-and-information-shocks]]). The apparent contradiction — rigorous natural experiments producing opposite signs — is the empirical puzzle the debate must resolve, and it is what shifts attention from *which model is right* to *when each holds*.

## Reconciling the models: scope conditions

The reconciliation hinges on recognising that the two models describe different margins that dominate under different conditions. The pivotal result comes from jointly modelling agency problems and external-financing constraints: firms with high external-financing constraints *decrease* payout as governance improves (the substitute margin — better governance lets them retain cash and avoid costly issuance), while firms with low constraints *increase* payout as governance improves (the outcome margin — empowered outsiders extract free cash). The sign of the governance–payout relation reverses depending on the relative magnitude of agency costs versus external-financing costs ([[chae-2009-how-corporate-governance-affects-payout-policy-under-agency]]). This contingency also rationalises the divergent shocks. Board reforms strengthen internal monitoring and empower outsiders to demand cash, so payouts rise ([[bae-2021-board-reforms-and-dividend-policy-international-evidence]]); M&A laws substitute an external disciplinary mechanism — the takeover threat — for the payout commitment, so payouts fall ([[glendening-2016-the-market-for-corporate-control-and-dividend-policies]]); and information shocks lower the informational value of the payout commitment, so payouts also fall ([[hail-2014-dividend-payouts-and-information-shocks]]).

> **Cross-paper pattern:** The sign of the protection–payout relationship is not a fixed empirical fact but a function of two scope conditions — the firm's external-financing constraint relative to its agency costs, and whether the strengthened institution *empowers demanders* (board reform) or *substitutes for the payout's disciplinary role* (takeover threat, better information) ([[chae-2009-how-corporate-governance-affects-payout-policy-under-agency]]; [[glendening-2016-the-market-for-corporate-control-and-dividend-policies]]; [[hail-2014-dividend-payouts-and-information-shocks]]; [[bae-2021-board-reforms-and-dividend-policy-international-evidence]]).

## Current state of the debate

The modal empirical verdict favours the outcome model — the founding cross-section, the board-reform shock, and the causal direction of the laboratory experiment all point to protection raising payouts ([[laporta-2000-agency-problems-and-dividend-policies-around-the-world]]; [[bae-2021-board-reforms-and-dividend-policy-international-evidence]]; [[lariviere-2018-shareholder-protection-and-agency-costs-an-experimental-analysis]]). But the substitute logic has not been refuted; it survives as a scope-conditional bonding channel that dominates where firms face binding external-financing constraints or where a strengthened institution displaces rather than empowers the payout commitment ([[chae-2009-how-corporate-governance-affects-payout-policy-under-agency]]). Open questions concern mechanism substitution — how takeover threat, information environment, and legal rights trade off as alternative disciplinary devices — and heterogeneity in which margin binds for which firms.

> **Current assessment (2026-07):** The debate is best read not as "which model wins" but as "which margin dominates when." The outcome model is the modal verdict for the level relationship and for clean empowerment shocks; the substitute model holds where financing constraints bind or where a strengthened institution substitutes for the dividend's disciplinary role. The experimental finding that stronger protection simultaneously raises payouts, expropriation, and disinvestment is the sharpest reminder that a higher payout is not, by itself, evidence that outsiders are better off.
