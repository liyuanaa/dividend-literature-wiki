# Political and Policy Uncertainty and Payout

This page synthesizes the post-2015 wave of research on how macro-level and non-market uncertainty shapes corporate payout — economic policy uncertainty (EPU), political risk and crises, corruption, CEO political ideology, political freedom and democracy, pandemic shocks, and climate and carbon risk. The literature poses one question with two rival answers: does heightened uncertainty lead firms to *conserve* cash — a precautionary, flexibility-preserving response that cuts or reshapes payout — or to *disgorge* cash — a shielding, agency-mitigating response that raises payout and signals commitment to outside investors? The record turns out to be genuinely two-sided: high EPU raises dividends in international data, while political crises, climate risk, and firm-level cash-flow uncertainty cut or shift payout. The reconciliation is that the sign is conditional — on the type of uncertainty, its horizon and reversibility, firm free cash flow, and the strength of country institutions. This is the frontier of the payout literature, extending the classical determinants of distribution (agency, signaling, taxes, lifecycle) to a new class of macro and non-market risks that became salient after the global financial crisis and COVID-19. The sections below set up the conserve-versus-disgorge tension, then work through the uncertainty classes: economic policy uncertainty, political risk and corruption, political freedom and democracy, CEO ideology, the COVID-19 shock, and climate and carbon risk, before turning to reconciliation.

## Two rival responses: conserve versus disgorge

The organizing tension is between two logics that predict opposite payout responses to the same rise in uncertainty. The *precautionary/flexibility* logic holds that when the future becomes less predictable and external financing more costly, firms retain cash — cutting payout or shifting it toward reversible forms — to preserve the option to invest and to avoid distress. The *shielding/agency* logic holds the reverse: where uncertainty raises the risk that insiders, controlling shareholders, or a predatory environment will expropriate cash, firms distribute *more* to remove cash from harm's way and to mitigate agency conflict. The international EPU evidence sits on the disgorge side, reading high-uncertainty dividends as an agency-mitigation device ([[attig-2021-dividends-and-economic-policy-uncertainty-international-evidence]]); the political-crisis evidence sits on the conserve side, reading dividend termination as precautionary ([[huang-2015-political-risk-and-dividend-policy-evidence-from-international]]); and the corruption evidence adds a distinct geographic "shielding" channel in which a more predatory local environment raises payout ([[hossain-2021-political-corruption-and-corporate-payouts]]). The tension is not merely definitional — it forces the field to specify scope conditions rather than a single sign, and the moderators that flip it are free cash flow, governance, and country-level protection ([[attig-2021-dividends-and-economic-policy-uncertainty-international-evidence]]; [[huang-2015-political-risk-and-dividend-policy-evidence-from-international]]).

> **Cross-paper pattern:** Uncertainty does not have a single sign on payout. It *raises* distribution where the dominant threat is expropriation of retained cash (EPU as agency-mitigation, corruption as shielding) and *lowers or reshapes* it where the dominant threat is a costly future funding shortfall (political crises, climate risk, cash-flow uncertainty). Free cash flow, governance, and institutional protection are the conditions that determine which force dominates ([[attig-2021-dividends-and-economic-policy-uncertainty-international-evidence]]; [[huang-2015-political-risk-and-dividend-policy-evidence-from-international]]; [[hossain-2021-political-corruption-and-corporate-payouts]]).

## Economic policy uncertainty

Economic policy uncertainty — the unpredictability of governments' fiscal, regulatory, and monetary policy — is the most directly measured of the macro channels, and the first international evidence establishes a positive sign. Across 19 countries, a high level of EPU is positively associated with dividend payout, robust to alternative payout measures, to controlling for other sources of uncertainty, and to addressing endogeneity ([[attig-2021-dividends-and-economic-policy-uncertainty-international-evidence]]). The interpretation is agency-mitigating: dividends help discipline the use of cash during high-EPU periods, and consistent with this the effect is moderated by firms' free cash flow and governance quality and by the country-level quality of shareholder protection, disclosure, enforcement, and creditor protection ([[attig-2021-dividends-and-economic-policy-uncertainty-international-evidence]]). A tax-policy natural experiment supplies a complementary but distinct mechanism at the U.S. level: facing uncertainty around individual dividend-tax increases scheduled for 2011 and 2013, firms time the tax environment, initiating or intensively raising dividends one year before an expected increase, and — when uncertainty is lower in 2012 — favouring special dividends over commitment to regular ones ([[buchanan-2017-uncertainty-and-firm-dividend-policy-a-natural-experiment]]). The response is strongest in firms with high tax-affected ownership, supporting a "prepare for the worst" reading in which firms act to protect shareholders from an anticipated adverse policy shift ([[buchanan-2017-uncertainty-and-firm-dividend-policy-a-natural-experiment]]). The two studies together show that EPU's positive payout effect can arise from both agency-mitigation and tax-timing, and that the special-versus-regular dividend margin is itself a flexibility lever, connecting to [[financial-flexibility-and-payout]]. A related margin is the risk-transfer response: facing higher EPU, firms increase pension underfunding — shifting risk onto employees — and the effect is stronger for high-dividend-paying firms, suggesting that some firms preserve payout under uncertainty precisely by offloading risk elsewhere ([[cumming-2024-are-companies-offloading-risk-onto-employees-in-times]]).

## Political risk, crises, and corruption

Acute political risk pulls in the opposite direction from EPU, toward conservation. In a 35-country sample over 1990–2008, global political crises raise the market-perceived uncertainty and the cost of external financing, and using crisis events as a proxy for political risk, past dividend payers are more likely to terminate dividends and non-payers less likely to initiate them during high-political-risk periods — a precautionary response to political shocks ([[huang-2015-political-risk-and-dividend-policy-evidence-from-international]]). The effect is stronger for multinational corporations, whose exposure spans jurisdictions, but is attenuated by stabilizing institutions such as more stable political systems and stronger investor legal protection ([[huang-2015-political-risk-and-dividend-policy-evidence-from-international]]). Corruption operates through a different logic and yields the opposite sign: firms headquartered in more corrupt U.S. states pay out *more* to shareholders, a result robust to propensity-score matching, instrumental variables, headquarters relocations, and quasi-natural experiments, and interpreted as a geographic *shielding* channel in which distributing cash removes it from a predatory local environment ([[hossain-2021-political-corruption-and-corporate-payouts]]). The contrast is instructive: a discrete, potentially severe funding shock (a political crisis) triggers conservation, while a chronic expropriation-risk environment (corruption) triggers disgorgement — the same "non-market risk" heading, opposite payout responses, discriminated by whether the dominant threat is to future funding or to the safety of retained cash. Country institutions moderate both, linking this material to [[international-evidence-on-dividends]] and the [[investor-protection-and-payout]] debate.

## Political freedom, democracy, and institutions

At the level of country political institutions, the evidence again points toward disgorgement in worse environments but distinguishes the *value* of that payout. Firms in less politically free countries pay out more cash, consistent with low political freedom marking a less friendly investment environment; firms reduce payouts when a country's political freedom improves, and pay out past excess cash while cutting future investment when political freedom deteriorates ([[guedhami-2017-political-freedom-and-corporate-payouts]]). Critically, this higher payout in less-free countries is not mainly an agency-easing device: the cash payouts there are more volatile and hence less valuable, undercutting a clean disgorgement reading ([[guedhami-2017-political-freedom-and-corporate-payouts]]). Democracy sharpens the picture from the other side. Across 18,410 firms in 63 countries over 1991–2018, and using instrumental variables, democracy improves dividends, with the effect more pronounced for firms with high agency costs and in countries with weak legal protection for shareholders ([[bui-2025-democracy-dividends-and-corporate-valuation]]). Democracy-induced dividends are also capitalized at a higher rate — especially for high-growth firms — adding to corporate value beyond the premium associated with shareholder-rights-induced dividends ([[bui-2025-democracy-dividends-and-corporate-valuation]]). The two studies are not contradictory once the moderators are read carefully: political *un-freedom* is associated with more but lower-quality payout, while democratization raises payout that markets value more, both effects concentrated where agency costs and weak protection make dividends most useful as a commitment device.

## CEO political ideology

Not all political variation is at the country level; CEO political ideology introduces a firm-level, manager-driven channel. Measuring ideology by CEOs' political donations across S&P 500 firms over 1997–2019, conservative CEOs are more likely to pay dividends and to make share repurchases, and pay higher dividends, financing the higher distributions by drawing down cash holdings and reducing capital and R&D expenditure ([[bayat-2025-ceo-political-ideology-and-payout-policy]]). Importantly, ideology does *not* explain dividend cuts: firms led by conservative CEOs exhibit dividend flexibility comparable to those led by other CEOs, so the ideology effect operates on the level and initiation of payout rather than on the willingness to reverse it — leaving the flexibility option intact ([[bayat-2025-ceo-political-ideology-and-payout-policy]]). The COVID-19 period exposes the same disposition under stress: facing the pandemic, conservative CEOs were more likely to meet dividend expectations while aggressively reducing labour costs — using temporary downsizing to avoid earnings losses and thereby distributing the pandemic's pain onto the workforce rather than onto shareholders ([[bayat-2025-the-impact-of-ceo-political-ideology-on-labor]]). This firm-level channel is analytically distinct from the country-level uncertainty variables: it shows that *who* decides payout under uncertainty matters alongside the institutional environment in which the decision is made, and that a preference for maintaining shareholder distributions can be satisfied by shifting risk onto other stakeholders, echoing the pension-underfunding response ([[cumming-2024-are-companies-offloading-risk-onto-employees-in-times]]).

## The COVID-19 shock

The COVID-19 pandemic is the cleanest acute uncertainty event in the corpus, and it produced a sharp precautionary response that broke the normal smoothing of dividends. For major equity markets, the proportion of index value attributable to the first five years of dividends dropped substantially in the first quarter of 2020, and the drop was not reversed by year-end — a durable breakdown of dividend smoothing rather than a transient dip ([[cejnek-2021-the-covid-19-pandemic-and-corporate-dividend-policy]]). The cross-section of who cut identifies the precautionary logic: the breakdown was less severe for firms with higher operating cash flows and more positively coskewed returns, and more pronounced for firms with higher leverage and in the financial sector; heavy dividend cutters also experienced a substantial increase in exposure to systematic risk ([[cejnek-2021-the-covid-19-pandemic-and-corporate-dividend-policy]]). The severity for financial firms links directly to the regulatory dividend restrictions treated in [[bank-payout-and-prudential-regulation]], where the same event appears as a policy intervention rather than a firm choice. A dynamic-model rationalization is available from the flexibility literature: ambiguity aversion amplifies the precautionary motive and generates the elevated cash holdings and accelerated asset sales observed in the crisis ([[luo-2022-investment-payout-and-cash-management-under-risk-and]] via [[financial-flexibility-and-payout]]). The pandemic thus functions as a natural test of the conserve response — an unambiguous, severe, hard-to-reverse shock that pushed the cross-section toward cash retention in proportion to firms' exposure.

## Climate and carbon risk

Climate risk is the slow-moving counterpart to the acute pandemic shock, and it elicits a distinctive *reshaping* rather than pure cutting of payout. Across 45 countries, firms in high-climate-risk countries reduce cash dividends but increasingly use share repurchases, substituting one channel for the other to raise payout flexibility in response to heightened climate risk ([[chang-2024-climate-risk-and-payout-flexibility-around-the-world]]). Operating volatility and financial constraints are the two channels through which climate risk operates, and the effect is stronger for firms more vulnerable to climate risk and in countries where people are more concerned about it and where the national culture emphasizes uncertainty avoidance and long-term orientation ([[chang-2024-climate-risk-and-payout-flexibility-around-the-world]]). Carbon risk supplies quasi-experimental evidence with a tax-regime twist. In Australia's imputation environment, following ratification of the Kyoto Protocol the highest-emitting firms (polluters) reduce their probability of paying dividends and their payout ratio relative to non-polluters, and the reduction in payout ratio is smaller under the imputation system than under a classical tax system — highlighting that the tax environment conditions carbon risk's effect on payout *level* but not on the decision to pay ([[balachandran-2018-does-carbon-risk-matter-in-firm-dividend-policy]]). The post-Kyoto reduction is driven by polluters' relative increase in earnings uncertainty, giving carbon risk a clean causal route into payout via cash-flow uncertainty ([[balachandran-2018-does-carbon-risk-matter-in-firm-dividend-policy]]).

> **Cross-paper pattern:** Under both the acute COVID-19 shock and the slow-moving climate/carbon channel, the recurring response is a precautionary shift — cutting cash dividends (COVID, Kyoto polluters) or substituting reversible repurchases for committed dividends (climate risk) — that preserves flexibility rather than a wholesale exit from payout ([[cejnek-2021-the-covid-19-pandemic-and-corporate-dividend-policy]]; [[chang-2024-climate-risk-and-payout-flexibility-around-the-world]]; [[balachandran-2018-does-carbon-risk-matter-in-firm-dividend-policy]]).

## Open questions and reconciliation

The central open question is when uncertainty raises versus cuts payout, and the emerging answer organizes the evidence by the nature of the threat rather than by a single elasticity. Country-level environments where the dominant risk is expropriation of retained cash — high EPU under weak governance, corruption, low political freedom — tend to raise payout as a shielding or agency-mitigation device ([[attig-2021-dividends-and-economic-policy-uncertainty-international-evidence]]; [[hossain-2021-political-corruption-and-corporate-payouts]]; [[guedhami-2017-political-freedom-and-corporate-payouts]]). Acute, hard-to-reverse funding shocks where the dominant risk is a future shortfall — political crises, the pandemic, rising earnings uncertainty from carbon exposure — tend to cut or reshape payout toward reversible forms ([[huang-2015-political-risk-and-dividend-policy-evidence-from-international]]; [[cejnek-2021-the-covid-19-pandemic-and-corporate-dividend-policy]]; [[balachandran-2018-does-carbon-risk-matter-in-firm-dividend-policy]]). Whether the international EPU result survives a fully precautionary framework — that is, whether the positive sign is agency-mitigation or a composition effect of which firms pay under uncertainty — remains contested, and the moderating roles of horizon, reversibility, and institutions are still being mapped ([[attig-2021-dividends-and-economic-policy-uncertainty-international-evidence]]; [[chang-2024-climate-risk-and-payout-flexibility-around-the-world]]).

> **Current assessment (2026-07):** The field has moved from asking whether uncertainty affects payout to specifying *which* uncertainty and *under what conditions*. The best-supported reconciliation is that the sign depends on the type of uncertainty (expropriation-risk versus funding-risk), the horizon and reversibility of the shock, firm free cash flow, and the strength of country institutions — with country-level predation risk favouring disgorgement and acute funding shocks favouring conservation. The firm-level precautionary motive documented in [[financial-flexibility-and-payout]] is the through-line, and CEO-level channels show that who decides matters alongside where. What is not yet settled is a unified model that predicts the sign ex ante from observable features of the shock and the institutional setting.
