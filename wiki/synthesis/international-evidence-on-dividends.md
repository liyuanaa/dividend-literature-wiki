# International Evidence on Dividends

Cross-country research is where the dividend literature turns the fixed institutional backdrop of a single market into a set of variables. Within one country the level of investor protection, the tax regime, and the typical ownership structure are constants, so a determinant of payout cannot be separated from the institutions that surround it; exploiting variation across nations in law, taxation, ownership, and disclosure makes those constants into regressors and turns cross-country data into the natural laboratory for testing dividend theory. The organizing insight of this literature, from La Porta, Lopez-de-Silanes, Shleifer and Vishny, is that dividends are shaped by how well legal regimes protect minority shareholders — the "outcome" versus "substitute" framing that anchors the entire body of work and whose master variable is the common-law/civil-law divide of legal origin. Around this axis the field integrates evidence on the lifecycle determinants of paying, tax regimes, ownership concentration and expropriation, disclosure and earnings quality, dividend smoothing, catering, and the worldwide decline in the propensity to pay. The recurring payoff is a verdict on which US-based "puzzles" replicate globally and which are regime-specific: agency and lifecycle explanations travel, whereas signaling, catering, and disappearing dividends turn out to be conditional on the legal environment. The legal-origin thread runs *through* every sub-theme below rather than sitting in a section of its own.

## The legal-origin / investor-protection axis

The foundational test pits two agency models of dividends against each other. Under the *outcome model*, dividends are paid because minority shareholders with legal power pressure insiders to disgorge cash, so stronger minority rights predict *higher* payouts; under the *substitute model*, insiders who expect to return to equity markets pay dividends to build a reputation for fair treatment, so stronger rights predict *lower* payouts ([[laporta-2000-agency-problems-and-dividend-policies-around-the-world]]). The cross-section of roughly 4,000 companies from 33 countries with differing minority-rights regimes supports the outcome model: better-protected shareholders extract more, not less ([[laporta-2000-agency-problems-and-dividend-policies-around-the-world]]). This makes legal origin — the common-law regimes that tend to protect minorities strongly versus the civil-law regimes that protect them weakly — the master variable of the field, and it recurs as an interaction term throughout later work on taxation, disclosure, catering, and the propensity to pay ([[alzahrani-2012-investor-protection-taxation-and-dividends]]; [[ferris-2009-catering-effects-in-corporate-dividend-policy-the-international]]; [[brockman-2011-earned-contributed-capital-dividend-policy-and-disclosure-quality]]). The long-run record adds a note of caution about how forcefully the axis operates through *legislative change*: across all Belgian firms listed in Brussels over 1838–2012 — a period spanning very weak pre-WWI protection, its gradual improvement, and the 1920 introduction of dividend taxation — dividend policy is remarkably stable and changes in investor-protection and tax legislation had little impact, suggesting the cross-sectional protection–payout link need not translate into strong within-country time-series responses to legal reform ([[moortgat-2017-investor-protection-taxation-and-dividend-policy-long-run]]).

> **Cross-paper pattern:** The cross-country evidence converges on the outcome model — stronger minority-shareholder protection is associated with higher payout — with legal origin (common vs. civil law) as the organizing axis, though the long-run Belgian record shows the association is weaker as a within-country response to legislative change than as a cross-sectional regularity ([[laporta-2000-agency-problems-and-dividend-policies-around-the-world]]; [[brockman-2011-earned-contributed-capital-dividend-policy-and-disclosure-quality]]; [[moortgat-2017-investor-protection-taxation-and-dividend-policy-long-run]]).

## Lifecycle and the determinants of paying

The most robustly replicated cross-country regularity is the lifecycle pattern of who pays. Across the US, Canada, UK, Germany, France, and Japan, the propensity to pay dividends is higher among larger and more profitable firms and those for which retained earnings make up a large fraction of total equity — the earned/contributed-capital signature of the lifecycle theory ([[denis-2008-why-do-firms-pay-dividends-international-evidence-on]]). The worldwide-decline study reaches the same cross-sectional verdict in a far larger sample of more than 17,000 firms from 33 countries: larger firms, more profitable firms, and firms with low growth opportunities are more likely to pay ([[fatemi-2012-yes-dividends-are-disappearing-worldwide-evidence]]). The lifecycle account is not merely mechanical maturity; it carries an agency component that the disclosure environment activates, so that the earned/contributed-capital pattern holds around the world *and* the firm's disclosure environment shapes payout through its effect on agency costs — an agency-cost-inclusive lifecycle theory ([[brockman-2011-earned-contributed-capital-dividend-policy-and-disclosure-quality]]). The lifecycle determinants also survive controlling for the cash-flow-uncertainty channel that operates worldwide, underscoring that maturity and risk are distinct forces (see the uncertainty treatment in [[financial-flexibility-and-payout]]). This convergence is what lets the international record be read as *support* for agency/lifecycle theories where it *casts doubt* on signaling and catering ([[denis-2008-why-do-firms-pay-dividends-international-evidence-on]]).

> **Cross-paper pattern:** Size, profitability, and a high earned/contributed-capital (retained-earnings) mix are the robust global determinants of the propensity to pay, replicating across six developed markets and 33-country samples, with the disclosure environment adding an agency-cost overlay ([[denis-2008-why-do-firms-pay-dividends-international-evidence-on]]; [[fatemi-2012-yes-dividends-are-disappearing-worldwide-evidence]]; [[brockman-2011-earned-contributed-capital-dividend-policy-and-disclosure-quality]]).

## Taxation across regimes

Cross-country variation in tax systems exposes how the dividend/repurchase channel choice responds to the tax wedge, and it does so *interacted with* investor protection rather than uniformly. Under a classical tax system, firms in strong-investor-protection countries pay *lower* cash dividends than firms in weak-protection countries but repurchase more shares to maximize shareholders' after-tax returns — the reverse of the naive protection–payout prediction once the tax cost of cash dividends is admitted ([[alzahrani-2012-investor-protection-taxation-and-dividends]]). In weak-protection countries both cash dividends and repurchases are low and *less responsive* to taxes: protected investors weigh the tax cost of dividends against the agency-cost benefit of disgorgement and shift toward repurchases, while unprotected investors accept whatever they can extract even at high tax cost ([[alzahrani-2012-investor-protection-taxation-and-dividends]]). This tax–protection interaction refines rather than overturns the outcome model, and it connects to the lifecycle determinants because the earned-capital firms most likely to pay are also those with the flexibility to substitute repurchases for dividends ([[denis-2008-why-do-firms-pay-dividends-international-evidence-on]]). The long-run Belgian evidence again tempers expectations about the time-series force of taxation: the 1920 introduction of dividend taxation left dividend policy largely unmoved, so the strong cross-sectional tax responsiveness is not mirrored by a sharp response to the arrival of a tax ([[moortgat-2017-investor-protection-taxation-and-dividend-policy-long-run]]). Tax regimes also condition specific event-driven results, such as the imputation-versus-classical contrast in how carbon risk affects payout, treated in [[political-and-policy-uncertainty-and-payout]].

## Ownership, expropriation, and weak institutions

Where ownership is concentrated and external institutions are weak, dividends take on a role as a substitute for the institutional protections that are missing. Concentrated controlling shareholders can expropriate minority investors, and the value of the cash the firm holds falls accordingly: the relation between cash holdings and firm value is much weaker in poor-investor-protection countries, consistent with controlling shareholders extracting private benefits from retained cash, and the relation between dividends and firm value is itself weaker where protection is stronger ([[pinkowitz-2006-does-the-contribution-of-corporate-cash-holdings-and]]; [[laporta-2000-agency-problems-and-dividend-policies-around-the-world]]). In this setting firms use the *timing* and commitment of payout as a bonding device: in weak-institution countries firms display a higher speed of adjustment to their target payout ratio, pay dividends earlier in the lifecycle, and are more likely to disclose a policy committing to pay a minimum percentage of earnings — a commitment that reveals the extent of firm-level agency conflict to future investors and facilitates raising external capital ([[ellahie-2021-show-me-the-money-dividend-policy-in-countries]]). Within weak-institution countries, the firms with the highest-speed-of-adjustment policies have fewer agency problems and greater ability to raise capital, and their earnings announcements draw larger market reactions — evidence that the payout commitment is read as informative precisely where institutions cannot be relied on ([[ellahie-2021-show-me-the-money-dividend-policy-in-countries]]). The same logic underlies the earnings-quality results: payers manage earnings less to establish credible reputations and ease access to external funds, and the effect is strongest where institutions are weak ([[he-2017-dividend-policy-and-earnings-management-across-countries]]). Cross-border regulatory cooperation can substitute for domestic institutional reform in the same vein: firms cross-listed in the U.S. from countries that joined the Multilateral Memorandum of Understanding (MMoU) securities-enforcement arrangement showed significant post-entry increases in dividend payouts, with effects concentrated in high-agency-cost firms from countries with weak external governance and opaque disclosure — consistent with strengthened cross-border enforcement reducing expropriation and supporting the outcome model even absent domestic legal change ([[chen-2022-cross-border-enforcement-of-securities-laws-and-dividend-payouts]]).

## Disclosure, transparency, and earnings quality

The information environment is a cross-country moderator in its own right, and it carries the outcome-versus-substitute tension into the disclosure domain. The *outcome hypothesis* predicts that transparent disclosure raises payout because shareholders can more accurately measure and demand excess cash; the *substitute hypothesis* predicts that opaque disclosure raises payout because managers have stronger incentives to build a fair-treatment reputation — and the international disclosure evidence confirms *both*, delivering the agency-cost-inclusive lifecycle theory in which disclosure shapes payout through agency costs ([[brockman-2011-earned-contributed-capital-dividend-policy-and-disclosure-quality]]). Earnings quality moves in tandem: across 23,429 firms from 29 countries, dividend payers manage earnings *less* than non-payers, and the gap is stronger in countries with weak investor protection and high opacity, with payers managing earnings even less when they issue equity following dividends — a pattern most pronounced in weak-institution, low-transparency settings ([[he-2017-dividend-policy-and-earnings-management-across-countries]]). The interpretation is that firms pair dividends with cleaner earnings to establish credible reputations and facilitate external financing, which dovetails with the commitment mechanism in weak-institution countries and with the tax–protection interaction, since disclosure quality and protection tend to co-move with legal origin ([[ellahie-2021-show-me-the-money-dividend-policy-in-countries]]; [[alzahrani-2012-investor-protection-taxation-and-dividends]]). Disclosure thus operates as the informational face of the same investor-protection axis that governs the level of payout.

## Dividend smoothing across countries

Lintner-style smoothing, long treated as a near-universal fact of dividend policy, turns out to vary systematically with institutions once the cross-country lens is applied. Across more than two thousand firms from 24 countries, managers of firms with low market-to-book ratios and less cash smooth dividends *more*, consistent with both agency and asymmetric-information motives operating simultaneously ([[javakhadze-2014-an-international-analysis-of-dividend-smoothing]]). Institutions shape the degree of smoothing in interpretable directions: firms with highly concentrated ownership and strong corporate governance smooth *less* — because the monitoring or control that concentration and governance provide substitutes for the commitment role smoothing otherwise plays — while firms in highly competitive industries smooth more ([[javakhadze-2014-an-international-analysis-of-dividend-smoothing]]). Beyond firm characteristics, the extent of legal protection, the tax regime, and national culture add explanatory power, so smoothing is jointly an agency, information, legal, and cultural phenomenon rather than a mechanical Lintner constant ([[javakhadze-2014-an-international-analysis-of-dividend-smoothing]]). The cultural dimension is corroborated by behavioural evidence: a mental-accounting model relating payout to patience, loss aversion, and ambiguity aversion is verified across 29 countries and more than 43,000 firm-years, establishing behavioural patterns as direct determinants of dividend policy that earlier work could only reach indirectly ([[breuer-2014-the-behavioral-foundations-of-corporate-dividend-policy-a]]). Language structure adds yet another layer: firms in markets using languages with a weak future-time-reference (FTR) — where the grammar does not require speakers to distinguish present from future events (e.g., Chinese, Japanese, German) — pay systematically lower dividends than firms in strong-FTR markets (e.g., French, Italian), because weak-FTR speakers perceive future dividends as nearer and value them more highly, reducing the preference for immediate payout. Within-market quasi-experiments (Hong Kong after the 1997 sovereignty handover; German- vs. French-speaking Swiss regions) confirm the causal direction and show the effect is not driven by co-varying legal or institutional differences ([[he-zhang-2022-languages-and-dividends]]). Regional social trust offers a further regime-contingent cultural signal: in China's weak-institution environment, firms in low-trust regions pay *higher* dividends than those in high-trust regions, because low trust amplifies agency conflicts and shareholders demand dividends as a governance substitute — a pattern that inverts the positive trust–dividend relationship found in the U.S. where formal institutions are strong and trust reinforces shareholder confidence, with the direction of the relationship depending on the quality of the surrounding formal institutional backdrop ([[qin-2022-social-trust-and-dividend-payouts-evidence-from-china]]). Smoothing and its cultural determinants connect to the measurement tradition catalogued in [[lintner-model-and-smoothing-measures]].

## Catering and the worldwide decline in paying

Two prominent US findings — catering to a time-varying dividend premium, and the disappearance of dividends — are the sharpest cases of regime-conditionality. Dividend catering is present among firms in common-law countries but *not* among firms in civil-law countries, and it persists after controlling for firm lifecycle, so the catering channel operates only where the legal regime and its investor protections let shareholders both force dividends from managers and bid up the price of payers ([[ferris-2009-catering-effects-in-corporate-dividend-policy-the-international]]). Civil-law firms' relative failure to cater may reflect controlling shareholders' consumption of private benefits or indifference to temporary market misvaluation of their equity ([[ferris-2009-catering-effects-in-corporate-dividend-policy-the-international]]). The disappearing-dividends phenomenon is similarly uneven: there is a significant *worldwide* decline in the propensity to pay, driven mostly by smaller, less profitable firms with more investment opportunities, and it persists after controlling for firm characteristics — yet the decline in mean payout ratios and in the proportion of payers is much more pronounced in civil-law countries ([[fatemi-2012-yes-dividends-are-disappearing-worldwide-evidence]]). Denis and Osobov sharpen the contrast further: outside the US there is little evidence of a systematic positive relation between the relative prices of payers and non-payers and the propensity to pay, aggregate dividends have not declined, and abandonment by existing payers is economically unimportant except in Japan — findings that cast doubt on signaling, clientele, and catering explanations while supporting agency/lifecycle theories ([[denis-2008-why-do-firms-pay-dividends-international-evidence-on]]).

> **Cross-paper pattern:** Catering and disappearing dividends are legal-origin-conditional — catering appears in common-law but not civil-law regimes, and the propensity-to-pay decline is concentrated in civil-law countries — so both US "puzzles" are properties of the legal environment rather than universal features of payout ([[ferris-2009-catering-effects-in-corporate-dividend-policy-the-international]]; [[fatemi-2012-yes-dividends-are-disappearing-worldwide-evidence]]; [[denis-2008-why-do-firms-pay-dividends-international-evidence-on]]).

## What replicates and what is US-specific

Read as a whole, the international record functions as the strongest available test of competing dividend theories because it can hold up each theory against variation the US alone cannot supply. The agency and lifecycle accounts *travel*: the size/profitability/earned-capital determinants of paying replicate across developed and broad multi-country samples, the outcome model of protection and payout holds cross-sectionally, and the agency-cost-inclusive lifecycle theory is confirmed through the disclosure channel ([[denis-2008-why-do-firms-pay-dividends-international-evidence-on]]; [[laporta-2000-agency-problems-and-dividend-policies-around-the-world]]; [[brockman-2011-earned-contributed-capital-dividend-policy-and-disclosure-quality]]). By contrast the signaling, clientele, and catering explanations are *regime-conditional*: catering is confined to common-law countries, the price–propensity relation that would evidence catering or signaling is largely absent outside the US, and the disappearing-dividends trend is a civil-law phenomenon ([[ferris-2009-catering-effects-in-corporate-dividend-policy-the-international]]; [[denis-2008-why-do-firms-pay-dividends-international-evidence-on]]; [[fatemi-2012-yes-dividends-are-disappearing-worldwide-evidence]]). Even smoothing, the most Lintnerian of stylized facts, is conditional on ownership, governance, legal protection, tax, and culture rather than universal ([[javakhadze-2014-an-international-analysis-of-dividend-smoothing]]; [[breuer-2014-the-behavioral-foundations-of-corporate-dividend-policy-a]]).

> **Current assessment (2026-07):** The cross-country literature has largely settled that the agency/lifecycle theory is the robust global account of *why firms pay*, with investor protection (the outcome model) as its institutional master variable, while signaling, catering, and the disappearing-dividends trend are conditional on legal origin rather than universal. The tax channel refines the outcome model — strong-protection firms substitute repurchases for taxed dividends — and disclosure, ownership, and culture enter as moderators of the same protection axis. New evidence expands the cultural channel: language structure (future-time-reference) and regional social trust each carry residual explanatory power for dividend levels after controlling for legal indices, and their direction of effect on payout reverses depending on whether formal institutions are strong or weak — pointing to the quality of formal institutions as the variable that determines whether informal mechanisms amplify or substitute for governance pressure. Cross-border securities-enforcement cooperation (MMoU) produces payout effects analogous to domestic legal reform, and comply-or-explain regulations achieve mechanical payout compliance at measurable financing costs, both suggesting that the form and credibility of the enforcement mechanism matter as much as the existence of the statute. The open frontier is the endogeneity of institutions and the separation of culture from law, and how to reconcile forceful cross-sectional effects with the near-stability of dividend policy in long-run within-country records ([[laporta-2000-agency-problems-and-dividend-policies-around-the-world]]; [[alzahrani-2012-investor-protection-taxation-and-dividends]]; [[moortgat-2017-investor-protection-taxation-and-dividend-policy-long-run]]; [[he-zhang-2022-languages-and-dividends]]; [[qin-2022-social-trust-and-dividend-payouts-evidence-from-china]]).

## Open questions

The central unresolved problem is the endogeneity of institutions: legal origin, investor protection, tax regime, ownership structure, and disclosure quality are correlated with one another and with unobserved features of economic development, so isolating the causal contribution of any one is hard even with 33-country cross-sections ([[laporta-2000-agency-problems-and-dividend-policies-around-the-world]]; [[alzahrani-2012-investor-protection-taxation-and-dividends]]). Distinguishing culture from law is a related open question, since the behavioural determinants — patience, loss aversion, ambiguity aversion — and the legal-protection variables both track legal origin and both carry explanatory power for smoothing and payout ([[breuer-2014-the-behavioral-foundations-of-corporate-dividend-policy-a]]; [[javakhadze-2014-an-international-analysis-of-dividend-smoothing]]); language structure (FTR) and regional social trust now add further informal channels that do not reduce to the protection index but co-vary with it, sharpening the separation problem ([[he-zhang-2022-languages-and-dividends]]; [[qin-2022-social-trust-and-dividend-payouts-evidence-from-china]]). The effectiveness of regulatory design is a third open question: the comply-or-explain experience in China shows that minimum-payout targets can achieve compliance while degrading firm value and forcing debt issuance, raising the question of when mandatory payout rules protect minorities versus distort investment ([[he-li-2018-the-effects-of-a-comply-or-explain-dividend-regulation-in-china]]). Finally, the long-run and modern records must be reconciled: modern cross-country panels show strong protection and tax effects, yet the 1838–2012 Belgian evidence shows dividend policy barely responding to sweeping changes in investor protection and the introduction of taxation, raising the question of whether the cross-sectional associations reflect slow-moving equilibrium selection rather than responsiveness to institutional change ([[moortgat-2017-investor-protection-taxation-and-dividend-policy-long-run]]).
