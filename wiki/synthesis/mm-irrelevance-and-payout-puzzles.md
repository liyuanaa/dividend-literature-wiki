# MM Irrelevance and the Payout Puzzles

The theoretical spine of the payout literature is the Miller–Modigliani (1961) proposition that, in a frictionless capital market and holding investment policy fixed, the division of earnings between distribution and retention cannot affect firm value — investors can costlessly undo any payout choice through "homemade dividends." This irrelevance result is a benchmark, not a description of the world, and its enduring role is to force every positive theory of payout to name the specific friction it relaxes. Around that benchmark accreted a family of puzzles: Fischer Black's dividend puzzle (why pay taxed dividends at all?), the disappearing-dividends puzzle (why did payers vanish and then partly return?), and the substitution puzzle (what does the displacement of dividends by repurchases imply for irrelevance?). This page synthesizes how each friction — taxes, information asymmetry, agency costs, transaction costs, and the mere feasibility of retention — converts the irrelevance baseline into a determinate positive theory, and how a modern reappraisal of the theorem itself has reframed what MM does and does not claim. It carries no frontier section: its scope is foundational.

## The irrelevance benchmark

The MM logic rests on a separation: given a fixed investment program, the funds distributed today must be replaced by funds raised tomorrow (or forgone consumption financed by asset sales), so a shareholder indifferent to the timing of consumption can replicate any payout stream by trading on personal account. The homemade-dividend argument makes this precise: a shareholder who wants more cash than the firm distributes can sell a fraction of her holding, while one who wants less can reinvest the dividend, so that for a fixed investment policy the value $V_0$ equals the present value of the underlying project cash flows and is invariant to how those flows are split between a dividend $D_t$ and reinvestment. Value therefore depends on the earning power of assets, not on the packaging of the returns. The literature treats this as the correct point of departure while disagreeing sharply on what it licenses. The most influential reappraisal grants the algebra but denies the interpretation, arguing that MM's frictionless setup does not actually address the value comparison MM themselves posed ([[deangelo-2006-the-irrelevance-of-the-mm-dividend-irrelevance-theorem]]). A reconciling treatment accepts MM's perfect-market result as valid within its assumptions and locates the disagreement in what happens once repurchases and agency costs enter ([[handley-2008-dividend-policy-reconciling-dd-with-mm]]). The benchmark also has an empirical corollary that survives the theoretical dispute: managers behave as though distribution matters, treating the dividend level as on par with investment decisions rather than as a residual ([[brav-2005-payout-policy-in-the-21st-century]]). The value of the benchmark is diagnostic rather than descriptive — because it specifies the one world in which payout cannot matter, it turns the empirical question "why does payout appear to matter?" into the sharper question "which of MM's assumptions fails here?" Every downstream theory in the collection can be located by the assumption it relaxes: signaling relaxes symmetric information, agency theory relaxes the assumption that managers maximize shareholder value, clientele and catering models relax the frictionless-trading and rational-pricing assumptions, and the lifecycle account relaxes the assumption that retention is costless.

## The DeAngelo–DeAngelo critique

The sharpest challenge holds that the theorem is "irrelevant" in a double sense. The argument is that the joint effect of MM's assumptions is to mandate 100% free-cash-flow payout in every period: because retention that earns only the market rate creates no value and the assumptions foreclose value-destroying retention, "niggardly payouts" are infeasible and the firm's opportunity set is artificially constrained to policies that fully distribute free cash flow. Irrelevance then obtains, but in an economically vacuous sense, since the interesting comparison — generous versus niggardly payout — has been assumed away. Once the assumptions are relaxed to allow retention, payout policy matters in exactly the same sense that investment policy does ([[deangelo-2006-the-irrelevance-of-the-mm-dividend-irrelevance-theorem]]). The critique carries several constructive claims: the standard Fisherian model is empirically refutable and predicts that firms make large payouts in present-value terms; the present value of distributions equals the PV of project cash flows only when payout is optimized; and the NPV rule for investment is necessary but not sufficient for value maximization, requiring an analogous rule for payout. The verbal, logical character of this critique — it introduces no new formal model — is worth stressing, and the reconciling literature engages it on exactly those terms ([[handley-2008-dividend-policy-reconciling-dd-with-mm]]).

## Reconciling the two views

The reconciliation turns on two devices the original irrelevance statement underweights: stock repurchases and agency costs. Repurchases restore a channel through which a firm can distribute free cash flow without committing to a permanent dividend, which addresses the DeAngelo–DeAngelo insistence that full distribution be feasible; agency costs supply the reason retention is not value-neutral in practice, since cash left inside the firm can be diverted or wasted ([[handley-2008-dividend-policy-reconciling-dd-with-mm]]). On this reading MM's result and the DeAngelo–DeAngelo critique are not contradictory but describe different opportunity sets: perfect-market irrelevance holds within MM's assumptions, while payout relevance emerges the moment retention and its agency consequences are admitted ([[deangelo-2006-the-irrelevance-of-the-mm-dividend-irrelevance-theorem]]). Survey evidence is consistent with the reconciled picture: managers use repurchases as the flexible outlet for residual cash after investment while guarding the dividend as a commitment, and they express only weak support for the agency, signaling, and clientele stories in their pure forms ([[brav-2005-payout-policy-in-the-21st-century]]). The reconciliation matters beyond exegesis, because the two devices it introduces are exactly the ones that anchor the modern positive theory of payout: agency costs supply the free-cash-flow motive that the lifecycle literature builds on, and repurchases supply the flexible channel whose growth generated the substitution and disappearing-dividends puzzles treated below. In this sense the reconciliation is less a truce between two papers than the hinge connecting the frictionless benchmark to the whole empirical edifice — every friction-based theory can be read as specifying which of MM's assumptions it relaxes and what value consequence follows. The reconciliation matters for the field's self-image: if the two views were genuinely contradictory, the whole "MM plus a friction" research program would rest on an incoherent foundation, whereas if they describe different opportunity sets, the program is secure and the DeAngelo–DeAngelo critique becomes a clarification of scope rather than a refutation.

> **Cross-paper pattern:** The MM/DeAngelo–DeAngelo standoff dissolves once repurchases (a feasible full-distribution channel) and agency costs (a reason retention is not value-neutral) are added — the two devices Handley identifies as the reconciling mechanisms ([[handley-2008-dividend-policy-reconciling-dd-with-mm]]; [[deangelo-2006-the-irrelevance-of-the-mm-dividend-irrelevance-theorem]]; [[brav-2005-payout-policy-in-the-21st-century]]).

## Black's dividend puzzle and the frictions that resolve it

Black's puzzle — why firms pay dividends that were, historically, taxed more heavily than capital gains — presupposes that a value-maximizing firm should not pay them, and each candidate friction offers a different resolution. The critique above dissolves the puzzle at its root, arguing that it is a "non-puzzle" because it rests on the mistaken idea that MM's irrelevance theorem applies to payout/retention decisions, which it does not ([[deangelo-2006-the-irrelevance-of-the-mm-dividend-irrelevance-theorem]]). The empirical literatures then test which friction actually binds. Survey evidence weakly supports agency, signaling, and clientele motives and relegates taxes to a secondary role ([[brav-2005-payout-policy-in-the-21st-century]]); international evidence discriminates in favour of agency-cost lifecycle explanations and against signaling, clientele, and catering ([[denis-2008-why-do-firms-pay-dividends-international-evidence-on]]); and a historical experiment that strips away taxes, regulation, and institutional owners finds the surviving motive to be information-communication rather than agency, illiquidity, catering, or behavioural stories ([[turner-2013-why-do-firms-pay-dividends-evidence-from-an]]). That the resolutions differ by setting is itself informative: no single friction explains payout everywhere, which is why the puzzle spawned a family of theories rather than one winner.

Evidence from comprehensive Swedish firm-owner data refines the taxes-versus-other-frictions question by measuring how tax sensitivity interacts with agency and coordination frictions, rather than treating taxes as either primary or secondary in isolation. Absent frictions — in wholly owned closely held corporations where a single owner-manager faces aligned incentives — a dividend tax cut of roughly seven percentage points caused owner-managers to increase the dividend share of compensation by about 7.3 percentage points (a 69% rise relative to the pre-reform average), confirming that taxes have a first-order impact on payout in a nearly friction-free environment. But as agency issues and shareholder conflicts increase, this sensitivity is sharply muted: in firms with three owners, the response is 40% smaller; once a firm has five or more owners, the tax effect is negligible. Three mechanisms account for the attenuation: coordination costs among shareholders, heterogeneity in owners' tax preferences, and separation of ownership and control ([[jacob-michaely-2017-taxation-and-dividend-policy-the-muting-effect-of-agency-iss]]). The result reframes Black's puzzle: taxes are a powerful force on payout when uncorrupted by other frictions, but the interaction of ownership dispersion and agency costs explains why the tax channel appears secondary in survey and cross-sectional studies that capture the full, friction-ridden population of firms.

## The disappearing-dividends puzzle

The secular collapse in the fraction of dividend payers poses a distinct challenge to any value-based theory: if paying maximizes value for the right firms, why did so many stop? The founding decomposition shows the fall from 66.5% of firms in 1978 to 20.8% in 1999 owes partly to a firm population increasingly tilted toward small, unprofitable, high-growth new listings, but also to a genuine drop in propensity that holds regardless of characteristics and is at least as important as the compositional shift ([[fama-2001-disappearing-dividends-changing-firm-characteristics-or-lower-propensity]]). The puzzle deepened and then partly resolved with the reappearance after 2000: a later decomposition attributes half the original disappearance to characteristics and half to proclivity, and 82% of the reappearance to higher proclivity among stable, profitable firms, while noting that newly listed and delisted firms — not initiations and omissions by continuing firms — drive the trends ([[michaely-2022-disappearing-and-reappearing-dividends]]). Crucially, that same study finds the disappearance of total payout is far smaller and shorter-lived than the disappearance of dividends, pointing directly to substitution between dividends and repurchases ([[michaely-2022-disappearing-and-reappearing-dividends]]). The measurement questions underlying these decompositions are treated at [[payout-ratio-and-propensity-to-pay]]. What makes the disappearance a genuine puzzle rather than a mere fact is its tension with the lifecycle logic: if the pay decision tracks the earned/contributed capital mix and firm maturity, a wave of young, contributed-capital-heavy listings mechanically lowers the payer fraction without any firm changing its behaviour, yet the residual propensity decline shows that behaviour changed too ([[fama-2001-disappearing-dividends-changing-firm-characteristics-or-lower-propensity]]). International evidence complicates the "puzzle" framing further: outside the US aggregate dividends never declined and remained concentrated among the largest, most profitable firms, with any propensity dip driven by newly listed firms failing to initiate rather than by existing payers abandoning dividends ([[denis-2008-why-do-firms-pay-dividends-international-evidence-on]]). The puzzle is thus partly an artefact of the unit of analysis — firm counts versus aggregate dollars — a point the measurement page develops.

## The substitution puzzle

If repurchases have displaced dividends, then the "disappearance" may be a change of form rather than a change of substance — which bears directly on whether MM's irrelevance-of-form survives. The substitution hypothesis holds that firms finance repurchases with funds that would otherwise have raised dividends, so repurchases became the preferred vehicle for initiating a cash payout even as established firms preserved their dividends ([[grullon-2002-dividends-share-repurchases-and-the-substitution-hypothesis]]). By the late 2000s repurchases were the dominant payout form, dividend-only firms had become largely extinct, and the overall level of repurchases remained fundamentally anchored to earnings ([[skinner-2008-the-evolving-relation-between-earnings-dividends-and-stock]]). The taxonomy that emerged — firms that pay dividends and repurchase regularly, firms that only repurchase regularly, and firms that repurchase occasionally — implies that "the payout decision" is really two decisions, a commitment margin and a flexible margin, and only the former resembles the sticky dividend of the mid-century literature ([[skinner-2008-the-evolving-relation-between-earnings-dividends-and-stock]]). The decisive reframing is that on a net-cash-distribution basis the decline vanishes: contrary to the dividend evidence, the conditional propensity to distribute net cash to equity holders has not declined over three decades, and net payout yields have actually risen ([[grullon-2011-has-the-propensity-to-pay-out-declined]]). This is the empirical fulcrum of the whole puzzle — it suggests total-payout relevance while leaving the irrelevance of the dividend/repurchase split an open possibility. It also inverts the apparent challenge to value theory: rather than firms retreating from distribution in a way a value-based account would struggle to explain, they substituted a more flexible channel for a rigid one while holding total returns to equity roughly constant, which is exactly what a friction-augmented MM model — repurchases feasible, retention costly through agency — would predict ([[grullon-2002-dividends-share-repurchases-and-the-substitution-hypothesis]]; [[grullon-2011-has-the-propensity-to-pay-out-declined]]).

> **Cross-paper pattern:** Measured as net cash to equity, aggregate payout did not fall — net payout yields rose while dividend incidence dropped — a stability that Grullon–Michaely, the later reappearance decomposition, and the earnings-anchored repurchase evidence all converge on ([[grullon-2011-has-the-propensity-to-pay-out-declined]]; [[michaely-2022-disappearing-and-reappearing-dividends]]; [[skinner-2008-the-evolving-relation-between-earnings-dividends-and-stock]]).

## A natural experiment on the frictions

Because the competing resolutions of Black's puzzle differ by institutional setting, a market that mechanically shuts off some frictions functions as a discriminating test. The Victorian London market between 1825 and 1870 had effectively zero tax rates, an unregulated capital market, and no institutional stockholders, allowing those determinants to be ruled out ex ante; in that setting share repurchases — though legal — were not used to return cash, and the evidence supports the information-communication explanation for dividends while offering little support for agency, illiquidity, catering, or behavioural accounts ([[turner-2013-why-do-firms-pay-dividends-evidence-from-an]]). This complements the contemporary cross-country test, which reaches a different verdict — favouring agency-cost lifecycle theories over signaling, clientele, and catering in modern developed markets ([[denis-2008-why-do-firms-pay-dividends-international-evidence-on]]). Read together, the two suggest the binding friction is not fixed across institutional environments, which is precisely why the puzzles have proven durable rather than decisively solved.

## Open questions and cross-links

The unresolved core is the status of irrelevance-of-form against the relevance of total payout. The net-payout evidence establishes that firms have not reduced what they return, only how they package it, which leaves open whether the dividend/repurchase mix is itself value-relevant or a matter of clientele and flexibility with no first-order effect on value ([[grullon-2011-has-the-propensity-to-pay-out-declined]]; [[grullon-2002-dividends-share-repurchases-and-the-substitution-hypothesis]]). A second open question is how far the DeAngelo–DeAngelo reframing should propagate through applied work that still invokes "irrelevance" loosely: if payout is relevant "in exactly the same sense that investment policy is," then the necessary condition for value maximization is a payout rule as well as an NPV rule, and empirical models that treat distribution as a pure residual are misspecified ([[deangelo-2006-the-irrelevance-of-the-mm-dividend-irrelevance-theorem]]; [[handley-2008-dividend-policy-reconciling-dd-with-mm]]). A third is whether the frictions that resolve Black's puzzle are stable across time and place or genuinely regime-dependent: the divergence between the Victorian information-communication verdict and the modern agency-lifecycle verdict suggests the binding friction shifts with the institutional environment, so that a single universal resolution to the dividend puzzle may not exist ([[turner-2013-why-do-firms-pay-dividends-evidence-from-an]]; [[denis-2008-why-do-firms-pay-dividends-international-evidence-on]]). These questions matter because nearly every other page in this wiki inherits a stance on them: the signaling, agency, catering, and clientele pages are each a claim about which friction converts the baseline into a positive theory.

> **Current assessment (2026-07):** The field's working consensus is that MM irrelevance survives as a benchmark and total-payout relevance is well established, but the irrelevance of the dividend-versus-repurchase form remains genuinely unsettled — the net-payout evidence is consistent both with pure form-neutrality and with form-relevance driven by flexibility and clientele frictions that the abstract-level evidence here cannot separate.
