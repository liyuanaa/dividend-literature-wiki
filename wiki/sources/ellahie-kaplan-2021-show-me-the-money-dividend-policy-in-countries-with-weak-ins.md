# Show Me the Money! Dividend Policy in Countries with Weak Institutions (2021)

> **Paper claim** — In countries with weak institutions, firms with less severe agency conflicts commit to returning a proportion of current earnings as dividends (high speed of adjustment), using dividend timing rather than level as a credible signal that facilitates external equity capital raising — a mechanism absent in strong-institution countries.

## Abstract

We hypothesize that, in weak-institution countries, firms adjust the "timing" of dividend payments by committing to distribute a percentage of current earnings as dividends, revealing the extent of firm-level agency conflicts to future investors and facilitating the raising of external capital. Consistent with this hypothesis, we find that, on average, firms in weak-institution countries have a higher speed of adjustment (SOA) to their target payout ratio, pay dividends earlier in the lifecycle, and are more likely to disclose a dividend policy committing to pay a minimum percentage of earnings. Within-country tests show that, in weak-institution countries, the firms with the highest SOA dividend policies have fewer agency problems and an increased ability to raise external capital. Finally, returns tests around earnings announcements show that high-SOA dividend policies are associated with larger market reactions to earnings in weak-institution countries. Collectively, our findings suggest that dividend policy helps to alleviate agency conflicts in weak-institution countries between firms and (future) investors.

## Key Findings

- Country-level Institutional Quality (World Bank governance indicators, normalized 0–1) explains 48.2% of cross-country variation in average dividend speed of adjustment (SOA) across 85 countries, with a coefficient of −0.42 (t = −7.61): firms in the weakest-institution country adjust dividends 42% more strongly in response to earnings changes than firms in the strongest-institution country.
- Firm-level cross-sectional regressions confirm the association (Institutional Quality coefficient = −0.33, t = −4.79 after controls), and the result is robust to the inclusion of mandatory dividend rules, ownership structure, tax regimes, and per capita GDP. SOA is also higher for gross payout (dividends plus buybacks) in weak-institution countries, though with 26% attenuation.
- Within-country tests show the SOA–Institutional Quality association is stronger for firms with higher market valuations, stronger analyst following, higher audit fees, lower return volatility, and greater equity issuance activity — consistent with high-SOA policies being adopted by firms with lower agency conflicts in weak-institution environments.
- 18% of the 349 largest dividend payers globally commit to a minimum payout ratio (average = 37% of earnings); firms with such policies are located in weaker-institution countries, have higher SOA, and adhere to their stated payout commitments.
- Cross-listed firms (US ADRs) in weak-institution countries exhibit lower SOA-Institutional Quality associations, consistent with US cross-listing bonding substituting for the commitment function of high-SOA dividend policy.
- Firms in weak-institution countries deviate from the typical life-cycle dividend pattern by paying dividends earlier (when growth options are still strong) and are more successful at equity capital raising when they maintain high-SOA policies (larger issuance amounts, more positive announcement returns).
