## Abstract

We decompose the decrease (1970s–2000) and subsequent recovery (2000–2018) in the fraction of dividend-paying firms. Changes in firm characteristics and proclivity to pay (probability of paying dividends conditional on characteristics) each drive half of the dividend disappearance. A higher proclivity drives 82% of the dividend reappearance. The remaining 18% is driven by a single characteristic: reduced earnings volatility. Changing characteristics are associated with low-profitability, high-earnings-volatility firms. Changing proclivity is associated with stable, profitable firms. Rather than dividend initiations or omissions, newly listed and delisted firms drive trends. Finally, the magnitude and duration of disappearing total payout is substantially smaller than that of dividends, indicating some substitution between dividends and repurchases.

## Key Findings

- The fraction of U.S. firms paying dividends fell from 73% in 1978 to 23% in 2000, then recovered to 36% by 2018; both the disappearance and reappearance are driven by newly listed and delisted firms, not by within-firm changes in dividend policy (consistent with Lintner dividend persistence).
- Disappearing dividends are attributable roughly equally to changing firm characteristics (52.6% — an influx of low-profitability, high-earnings-volatility firms) and changing proclivity to pay (47.4% — profitable, stable firms increasingly choosing not to pay).
- Reappearing dividends are driven overwhelmingly by changing proclivity (81.7%), with the remaining 18.3% attributable entirely to reduced earnings volatility — the only characteristic that moved in the direction of reappearance.
- The reappearance is mechanically driven by non-dividend-paying firms delisting (primarily via mergers/acquisitions); if M&A delistings did not occur post-2000, dividends would have reappeared only one-third as much.
- Total payout (dividends + repurchases) showed a smaller and shorter-lived gap (trough −9.1 pp, gap closes by 1998) than dividends alone (trough −21.7 pp), indicating substantial substitution of repurchases for dividends; repurchases continue growing even as dividends reappear, consistent with the passage of SEC rule 10b-18 in 1982.
