## Abstract

We use variation in real estate prices as exogenous shocks to firms' debt capacity to study the causal effect of financial flexibility on payout policy. We show that an increase in financial flexibility results in higher dividends, share repurchases, and payout flexibility. We find that a 1-standard-deviation increase in a firms' collateral value results in 0.26- and 0.55-percentage-point increases in nondiscretionary and discretionary payouts, respectively. This effect is stronger for firms with few investment opportunities. Moreover, highly leveraged firms are more likely to cut dividends in response to a sharp decrease in their financial flexibility.

## Key Findings

- A $1 increase in real estate collateral value is associated with a 0.30-cent increase in cash dividends (OLS); a 1-standard-deviation increase in pledgeable collateral translates to a 0.26-percentage-point increase in nondiscretionary payout (7.0% of a standard deviation) and a 0.55-percentage-point increase in discretionary payout (9.5% of a standard deviation), consistent across OLS and IV specifications using land supply elasticity × interest rates as instrument.
- An increase in financial flexibility leads to higher share repurchases in addition to higher dividends; payout flexibility (ratio of share repurchases to total payout) also increases with collateral value, consistent with firms allocating more of the temporary increases in financial flexibility to share repurchases than to dividends.
- During the 2008–2011 real estate bust, highly leveraged firms are significantly more likely to cut dividends in response to declining financial flexibility (coefficient on BUST_DUMMY × HIGH_LEVERAGE_DUMMY × RE_VALUE is positive and significant), while low-leverage firms maintain dividend payments.
- The effect of financial flexibility on dividends and share repurchases is concentrated in firms with few investment opportunities (low Tobin's Q); among these, financially constrained firms make even greater use of the collateral channel to increase payouts.
- Transmission operates via two channels: (i) the collateral channel — increases in CRE value relax borrowing constraints and allow firms to raise debt to fund payouts; (ii) the substitution channel — firms substitute future real estate services with current payouts.
