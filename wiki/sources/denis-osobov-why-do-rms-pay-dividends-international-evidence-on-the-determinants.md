# Why do firms pay dividends? International evidence on the determinants of dividend policy (2008)

> **Paper claim** — Across six developed markets, the propensity to pay dividends is driven by firm size, profitability, and the earned/contributed equity mix; any observed declines in dividend propensity are concentrated among newly listed firms that fail to initiate; and aggregate dividends remain concentrated among the largest, most profitable firms — evidence that supports life-cycle theory while casting doubt on signaling, clientele, and catering explanations.

## Abstract

In the US, Canada, UK, Germany, France, and Japan, the propensity to pay dividends is higher among larger, more profitable firms, and those for which retained earnings comprise a large fraction of total equity. Although there are hints of reductions in the propensity to pay dividends in most of the sample countries over the 1994–2002 period, they are driven by a failure of newly listed firms to initiate dividends when expected to do so. Dividend abandonment and the failure to initiate by existing nonpayers are economically unimportant except in Japan. Moreover, in each country, aggregate dividends have not declined and are concentrated among the largest, most profitable firms. Finally, outside of the US there is little evidence of a systematic positive relation between relative prices of dividend-paying and non-paying firms and the propensity to pay dividends. Overall, these findings cast doubt on signaling, clientele, and catering explanations for dividends, but support agency cost-based lifecycle theories.

## Key Findings

- In logit regressions across all six countries (1989–2002), the probability of paying dividends is positively and significantly associated with firm size, profitability, and the ratio of retained earnings to total book equity (RE/BE) — with RE/BE showing a near-monotonic relationship from near-zero payer proportions when RE/BE < 0 to above 85% when RE/BE > 0.90.
- Any declines in dividend propensity are driven predominantly by the failure of newly listed firms (post-1993 entrants) to initiate dividends when expected to do so; this subcategory accounts for more than 80% of the shortfall in dividend payers in 2002 in each country except Japan. Abandonment by existing payers is economically trivial except in Japan, where financial distress explains the pattern.
- Aggregate real dividends did not decline over 1990–2002 in any country except Canada; they remain highly concentrated among the largest payers (top 20% of payers account for at least 73.3% of aggregate dividends in all periods and countries), consistent with the life-cycle theory's prediction that free cash flow distribution drives dividends.
- Outside the US, there is little evidence that the Baker–Wurgler dividend premium drives the propensity to pay dividends or time-series changes therein, providing weak international support for the catering theory.
- The pattern that dividends are paid by the largest, most profitable firms — precisely those least in need of signaling — casts doubt on signaling as a first-order determinant of dividends internationally.
