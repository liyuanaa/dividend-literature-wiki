## Abstract

Dividends are clustered in increments of 5, such as 25, 50, and 75. Firms that gravitate towards these "prominent" amounts have lower operating performance and lower annual five-factor alphas of 77 b.p. Consistent with agency frictions that lead to lazy decisions, clustering effects are stronger for entrenched firms, with more market power, and less shareholder activism. Dividend increases also cluster more than cuts, consistent with saliency bias. In a counterfactual exercise, we find no similar patterns in a sample of ADRs. Our results complement a number of recent studies showing the economic importance of simple decision heuristics.

## Key Findings

- Non-prominent dividend payers earn 6.4 b.p. higher monthly 12-month alpha than prominent payers (significant at 10%); 24- and 36-month alphas are 6.7 and 6.4 b.p. higher (significant at 5%); matched-sample difference reaches 27 b.p. per month.
- Non-prominent dividend payers have significantly higher industry-adjusted Tobin's q (coefficient 0.03) and annual ROA approximately 0.7%–1.3% higher, indicating the effect extends to accounting-based performance measures.
- Prominent dividend clustering is stronger for industry-dominant firms (market power proxy for managerial quiet life), for firms with lower institutional ownership, and for firms with fewer outside directors—three proxies consistent with reduced managerial effort under weak monitoring.
- Dividend initiations are significantly more prominent than other dividends (+6 percentage points), while cuts are not, consistent with initiations being open numerical judgments that anchor on round numbers without a prior reference value.
- Special dividends (both explicitly flagged and algorithm-identified) are substantially less prominent (23%–35%) than regular dividends (39%), consistent with deliberate payout decisions being less lazy.
- Aggregate dividend prominence has declined from ~60% in 1963 to ~30% in 2018, coinciding with the secular rise in institutional ownership; time-series regressions confirm a significant negative association with aggregate institutional ownership.
- ADR placebo tests show no significant performance difference between prominent and non-prominent ADR dividends, isolating the effect to voluntary decimal-place choices by U.S. managers.
