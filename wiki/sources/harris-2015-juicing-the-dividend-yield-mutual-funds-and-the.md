## Abstract

Some mutual funds purchase stocks before dividend payments to artificially increase their dividends, which we call "juicing." Funds paid more than twice the dividends implied by their holdings in 7.4% of fund-years examined. Juicing is associated with larger inflows, and is more common among funds with unsophisticated investors. This behavior is consistent with an underlying investor demand for dividends, but is hard to explain by taxes or need for income, as funds can generate equivalent tax-free distributions by returning capital. Juicing is costly to investors through higher turnover and increased taxes of 0.57% to 1.52% of fund assets per year. (C) 2015 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

## 摘要（中文）

一些共同基金在股利支付之前购买股票，以人为地增加其股利，我们称之为"催汁"（juicing）。在所考察的基金—年度中，有7.4%的基金支付的股利超过了其持股所隐含股利的两倍以上。催汁与更大的资金流入相关，并且在拥有非老练投资者的基金中更为常见。这种行为与投资者对股利的潜在需求相一致，但很难用税收或对收入的需求来解释，因为基金本可以通过返还资本产生等值的免税分配。催汁通过更高的换手率以及每年增加相当于基金资产0.57%至1.52%的税负而给投资者带来成本。

## Key Findings

- **7.4% of fund-years show extreme juicing** (dividends > 2× holdings-implied), and 28.5% show juicing beyond the 1% significance cutoff of the bootstrap distribution; juicing is persistent year-over-year, consistent with deliberate fund policy.
- **Juicing funds attract 6.8–12.2% more annual inflows** than comparable non-juicing funds, demonstrating investors reward the behavior and confirming an underlying psychological demand for dividend income rather than equivalent capital returns.
- Juicing is **significantly less likely in institutional share classes** and more common in high-expense funds, consistent with the catering explanation (unsophisticated investors) rather than a tax clientele (which predicts the opposite).
- Juicing funds hold shares that are on average **4.1 days closer to the next dividend payment**, and new holdings are 5.0 days closer, providing direct portfolio-level evidence of deliberate dividend-capturing trading.
- The strategy is **costly to investors**: higher portfolio turnover (11–17% per year) and annual tax costs of 0.57–1.52% of fund assets, while juicing funds do not generate higher returns and by some measures perform worse.
