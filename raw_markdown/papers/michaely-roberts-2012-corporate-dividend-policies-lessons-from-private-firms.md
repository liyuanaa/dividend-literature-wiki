# Corporate Dividend Policies: Lessons from Private Firms

- **Authors:** Michaely, Roni; Roberts, Michael R.
- **Journal:** Review of Financial Studies
- **Year:** 2012
- **DOI:** 10.1093/rfs/hhr108

## Abstract

We compare the dividend policies of publicly and privately held firms in order to help identify the forces shaping corporate dividends, and shed light on the behavior of privately held companies. We show that private firms smooth dividends significantly less than their public counterparts, suggesting that the scrutiny of public capital markets plays a central role in the propensity of firms to smooth dividends over time. Public firms pay relatively higher dividends that tend to be more sensitive to changes in investment opportunities than otherwise similar private firms. Ultimately, ownership structure and incentives play key roles in shaping dividend policies. (JEL G35, G32, G15)

## Review of Financial Studies, v25 n3 2012

### 1. Data

**Primary data source:** FAME database (Bureau Van Dijk) — accounting statements for all private and public companies in the United Kingdom, approximately 2.1 million total. Sample period: 1993–2002.

**Sample criteria:** Consolidated financial statements only; excludes LBOs, small firms (Companies House definition); excludes financial firms (SIC 6000–6999), utilities (SIC 4900–4939), agricultural firms, and public sector firms. Final sample: 8,751 firms, 69,651 firm-year observations.

**Ownership classification:**
- **Wholly Owned:** Fewer than 26 individually identified shareholders (median of 4 shareholders); owner-managers with minimal agency problems.
- **Private Dispersed:** "Too numerous to list" shareholders (typically > 20); dispersed ownership but private.
- **Public:** Publicly quoted on London Stock Exchange, OFEX, or AIM.

**Two samples for identification:**
1. Propensity score matched sample (Rosenbaum and Rubin 1983) — matched on size, profitability, leverage, sales growth, and industry.
2. Transition sample — firms that underwent IPO or going-private (MBO/LBO) during the sample period.

### 3. Dividend Smoothing

**Lintner (1956) partial adjustment model:** Estimated separately for each firm, requiring ≥ 8 observations.

**Speed of adjustment (α) — Table 3:**
- Wholly Owned: mean α = 0.83 (immediate response to earnings shocks)
- Private Dispersed: mean α = 0.63
- Public: mean α = 0.41 (smoothest — smallest fraction of earnings shock transmitted immediately)

**Dividend changes — Table 2 (Matched Sample):**
- Wholly owned omit dividends 8.0% of the time vs. 3.7% for public firms.
- Both private firm groups cut dividends significantly more often than public firms.
- Public firms increase dividends 64% of the time (vs. 52% private dispersed, 26% wholly owned), but in much smaller amounts (roughly 1/4 and 1/10 respectively).
- Rate of dividend omission decreases by 56% and dividend cuts decrease by 40% when firms go from private to public (Transition Sample).

**Impulse response (Figure 1):**
- Wholly owned: immediately distributes ~88% of a £1 earnings shock; effect gone within 1 year.
- Private dispersed: immediately distributes ~63%; effect gone within 4 years.
- Public: immediately distributes ~41%; effect persists 6–7 years (smoothed).

### 4. Dividend Policy Characteristics

**Dividend levels — Table 5:**
- Public: 27% of operating profits (2% dividends/assets)
- Private dispersed: 17.8% of operating profits (0.9% of assets)
- Wholly owned: 13.4% of operating profits (0.7% of assets)

Consistent pattern in transition sample: firms pay higher dividends as public entities than as private (19.2% vs. 12.1% of operating profits).

**Dividend sensitivity to investment opportunities — Table 6:**
- Wholly owned: most negative coefficient on sales growth (most sensitive to investment needs): β = −0.122 (t = −2.0).
- Public: significantly negative: β = −0.073 (t = −3.4).
- Private dispersed: no significant sensitivity: β = −0.070 (not significant).

### 5. Discussion

**Signaling:** Smoothing among public firms is consistent with signaling to capital markets, but is unclear for private dispersed vs. public comparison.

**Taxes:** 1997 U.K. tax reform (withdrawal of pension fund dividend tax credits) did not change cross-group patterns, suggesting taxes cannot explain the variation.

**Costly external finance:** Private firms' lower dividends and greater dividend sensitivity to earnings are consistent with a costly external finance story (private firms value internal funds more highly).

**Owner diversification constraints:** Private owners' inability to easily sell shares might predict higher dividends to private firms, but the data show the opposite — evidence against diversification as a first-order explanation.

### 6. Conclusion

Public firms smooth dividends significantly more than private firms. The finding is consistent with: (1) public capital market scrutiny requiring commitment to stable dividends; (2) agency costs and information asymmetry playing a role, with wholly owned firms (minimal frictions) showing the least smoothing and highest earnings sensitivity. Ownership structure and the associated incentive environment are key determinants of dividend policy beyond taxes or investment opportunities.
