# Multiple Large Shareholders and Dividends: Evidence from China

- **Authors:** Jiang, Fuxiu; Cai, Xinni; Jiang, Zhan; Nofsinger, John R.
- **Journal:** Pacific-Basin Finance Journal
- **Year:** 2019
- **DOI:** 10.1016/j.pacfin.2019.101201

## Abstract

Faccio et al. (2001) find that when East Asian firms have multiple large shareholders, they will collude to expropriate wealth and withhold dividend payouts. However, when it becomes difficult for large shareholders to expropriate wealth through activities like tunneling, large shareholders may then need dividends to fund personal cash flow needs. Thus, they may cooperate to make firms pay dividends and pay large dividends. To test this hypothesis, we study Chinese-listed firms. Consistent with our contention, we find that firms with multiple large shareholders are more likely to pay dividends and pay large dividends. These activities are especially prevalent after restrictions were placed on tunneling. We also find that dividend payouts and large payouts are more likely when the largest shareholder needs the cooperation of other blockholders to exert control over dividend payout policy, and when multiple blockholders are of the same identity.

## 1. Introduction

The ownership structure of Chinese listed firms is known for being highly concentrated, usually with a large shareholder who may own enough shares to control the firm. Jiang and Kim (2015) show that for Chinese A-share listed firms, the average proportion of the firm held by the largest shareholder is over 35% during the period from 1998 to 2012.

Large shareholders in China desire dividends for several reasons. First, large shareholders are often long-term owners who are either prohibited from selling shares, or are reluctant to sell shares because it will reduce their control rights and possible private benefits. Therefore, large shareholders are left with two other ways for realizing returns: (1) they could realize returns by extracting private rents through tunneling or other questionable means, or (2) they could force firms to pay cash dividends.

Indeed, Chinese large shareholders were once well known to tunnel through "related-party transactions," which were later largely reduced by the mid-2000s. After tunneling through related-party transactions was banned, large blockholders needed an alternative source of cash, which we hypothesize to be dividends.

## 2. Institutional Background

### 2.1 Ownership Structure of Chinese Listed Firms

In China, shareholders with >10% ownership have the right to propose an interim shareholder meeting. In our sample, 73.9% of observations have only one large shareholder and the other 26.1% have multiple large shareholders (21.7% have two large shareholders, 3.8% have three large shareholders, and 0.6% have four or five large shareholders).

### 2.2 Dividend Policy in China

Dividends in China have several special features. First, the dividend yield (dividend to stock price ratio) is unusually low because of high price/earnings (P/E) ratios. The median P/E ratio and the median dividend yield are 38.6 and 0.56%, respectively, over our sample period. Second, China enjoys a high growth rate and thus has an abundance of valuable investment opportunities for firms. Third, unlike a stable dividend policy that is common in developed markets, Chinese firms' dividend payouts are relatively volatile.

## 3. Data, Variables, and Methodology

We obtain data from several sources. We start with all A-share companies listed on the Shanghai and Shenzhen Stock Exchanges over the period from 2000 to 2014. Following previous studies, we exclude firms in the financial sector because of their unique disclosure requirements and accounting rules. We also exclude firms with more liabilities than assets (i.e., leverage ratio higher than 1), firms with "special treatment" ("ST") status (i.e., distressed firms), and firms with negative dividend-to-earnings ratios (negative earnings). Following previous literature, we delete firms with no blockholders (i.e., widely-held firms), which are firms that do not have a shareholder owning at least 10% of the firm.

This leaves us with a large sample consisting of 20,516 firm-year observations for 2428 non-financial firms over a period of 15 years, from 2000 to 2014.

We define a shareholder as "large" or as a "blockholder" if the shareholder owns 10% or more of the firm's outstanding shares. We define an indicator variable, Multi, to be equal to 1 if the firm has two or more blockholders in that year, and 0 otherwise.

### Dependent Variables

Three measures for cash dividend payouts:
1. Ifdiv: indicator variable equal to 1 if total cash dividends of the year are more than zero
2. Div_e: dividend payout ratio (total cash dividends / earnings)
3. Div_ta: total cash dividends / total assets

## 4. Multiple Blockholders and Tunneling

The coefficient for Multi is significantly positive for both versions of related-party transactions variables in the first subsample (2000 to 2007). In the second subsample (2008 to 2014), the Multi coefficients are negative and not statistically significant. This suggests that blockholders are not acting together for tunneling activities during the second half of the sample.

## 5. Multiple Blockholders and Dividend Payouts

### 5.1 Main Results

The marginal effect of Multi on dividend propensity (Ifdiv) is 2.8% — a 2.8% higher propensity to pay cash dividends in multiple blockholder firms. The marginal effect of Multi from the Tobit regression for Div_e (Div_ta) is 0.0325 (0.0015), which is around 12% (11%) of the unconditional average value. The effect of multiple blockholders on cash dividend payouts is both economically large and statistically significant.

Sub-period results show that the sizes of the coefficients on Multi are larger in the second sub-period (2008–2014) compared to the first sub-period (2000–2007), indicating that when large shareholders have limited ability to tunnel, they exert greater force on firms to pay dividends.

### 5.2 Robustness Tests

Results are robust to two-stage least squares regression (using province-level average Multi as instrument), difference-in-difference tests (firms transitioning from multiple to single blockholder experience decreases in dividends), propensity score matching, and controlling for shareholder liquidity preferences.

## 6. Under What Conditions Will Multiple Blockholders Cooperate?

### 6.1 Need for Cooperation from Multiple Blockholders

Dividend payout is larger when other blockholders own more shares relative to the largest shareholder. Propensity to pay dividends is higher and dividend amount is larger when the largest shareholder can gain control of the firm through, and only through, cooperation with other large shareholders.

### 6.2 Does the Identity of the Blockholders Make a Difference?

When other blockholders are of the same identity as the largest shareholder, they are more likely to cooperate to force firms to pay dividends and pay large dividends. Specifically, private blockholders cooperate more strongly in private-controlled firms, while state blockholders cooperate more in state-controlled firms.

## 7. Does Forcing Higher Dividends Harm Minority Shareholders?

Traditional dividend theories predict that firms pay more dividends when they have poor growth opportunities. Multiple blockholders pay more cash dividends especially when faced with fewer investment opportunities, which is a reasonable payout policy predicted by prior literature. Through examining the cost of capital and dividend announcement effects, capital markets do not discount multiple blockholder firms for paying dividends. Thus, while the crackdown of related-party transactions forced large shareholders to form a negative coalition for more cash dividends, the cash dividends paid by multiple blockholder firms do not seriously damage minority shareholder wealth.

## 8. Conclusion

This paper reveals the relation between multiple large shareholders and dividends. We show a positive relation, which is in contrast to Faccio et al.'s (2001) findings of a negative relation and their collusion-to-expropriate hypothesis. Our positive relation suggests that blockholders are cooperating with one another to achieve a shared objective (obtaining cash dividends) rather than monitoring one another. We provide important additional evidence on the relation between multiple large shareholders and dividends, and shed new light by revealing another relational aspect of multiple large shareholders in general.
