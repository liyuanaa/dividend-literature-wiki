# The Effect of Financial Flexibility on Payout Policy

- **Authors:** Kumar, Anil; Vergara-Alert, Carles
- **Journal:** Journal of Financial and Quantitative Analysis
- **Year:** 2020
- **DOI:** 10.1017/S002210901800114X

## Abstract

We use variation in real estate prices as exogenous shocks to firms' debt capacity to study the causal effect of financial flexibility on payout policy. We show that an increase in financial flexibility results in higher dividends, share repurchases, and payout flexibility. We find that a 1-standard-deviation increase in a firms' collateral value results in 0.26- and 0.55-percentage-point increases in nondiscretionary and discretionary payouts, respectively. This effect is stronger for firms with few investment opportunities. Moreover, highly leveraged firms are more likely to cut dividends in response to a sharp decrease in their financial flexibility.

## 1. Introduction

Financial flexibility, a firm's ability to access financing in order to fund investment opportunities and unexpected expenses, has a significant impact on the decisions made by the firm's managers. In this article, we investigate the causal effect of financial flexibility on payout decisions. We use variation in real estate prices as an experiment that generates exogenous shocks to firms' debt capacity and, therefore, to their financial flexibility.

Most firms need real estate assets to run their businesses, and they need to choose between owning or leasing these assets. One of the most important characteristics of corporate real estate (CRE) assets is that they can be used as collateral to obtain debt financing. In a world without asymmetric information between firms' managers and lenders, the value of the assets that can be used for collateral would be irrelevant. However, in the presence of information frictions, these assets can be used as collateral to help reduce lending costs.

Our fundamental empirical analysis produces four sets of results:

1. An increase in financial flexibility leads to an increase in cash dividends paid to shareholders.
2. An increase in financial flexibility leads to more share repurchases.
3. Shocks to financial flexibility have different effects on dividends and share repurchases: an increase leads to more payout flexibility (ratio of share repurchases to total payout).
4. The impact of shocks to financial flexibility is higher for discretionary payouts than for nondiscretionary payouts. A 1-standard-deviation increase in pledgeable collateral translates to a 0.26-percentage-point increase in nondiscretionary payout (7.0% of a standard deviation) and a 0.55-percentage-point increase in discretionary payout (9.5% of a standard deviation).

## 2. Theoretical Predictions

Increases in real estate prices raise the level of financial flexibility and affect firms' payouts through two channels:

1. **Collateral effect**: Positive shocks to the value of CRE assets allow firms to increase their leverage in order to finance an increase in investments, and can shift part of the cash flow from investments to payout.
2. **Substitution effect**: Firms substitute capital from real estate services in the future with payouts in the present.

**Hypothesis 1**: Cash dividends increase in the market value of firms' collateralizable assets.
**Hypothesis 2**: Share repurchases increase in the market value of firms' collateralizable assets.
**Hypothesis 3**: Payout flexibility increases in the market value of firms' collateralizable assets.
**Hypothesis 4**: Highly leveraged firms are more likely to decrease their payouts in response to a decrease in the value of their collateralizable assets.
**Hypothesis 5**: The magnitude of the payout increase when the value of collateralizable assets increases is greater for firms with few investment opportunities.

## 3. Data

The sample contains 21 years of firm-level observations. Using all active firms listed in Compustat as of 1993, after exclusions (finance, insurance, real estate, construction, mining, non-US firms, firms present for fewer than 3 consecutive years), the final sample consists of 4,994 firms and 67,836 firm-year observations over 1993–2013.

We follow the methodology used by Chaney et al. (2012) to calculate the market value of corporate real estate assets using accumulated depreciation, historical cost of buildings, and state/MSA-level real estate price indices from the Federal Housing Finance Agency (FHFA). We also use Moody's/RCA commercial property price indices (CPPI) for city-level commercial real estate prices.

Summary statistics show that 35% of sample firms pay dividends, 41.7% repurchase shares, and 55.8% pay dividends or repurchase shares.

## 4. Empirical Strategy

The IV approach instruments local real estate prices using the interaction between land supply elasticity (Saiz 2010) and long-term interest rates, isolating variation in local real estate prices orthogonal to potentially omitted local economic variables.

To address the endogeneity of the decision to own real estate assets, we control for the interaction between initial firm-level characteristics (age, assets, ROA, 2-digit industry, state dummies) and contemporaneous real estate prices.

## 5. Main Results

### A. Effect of Financial Flexibility on Dividends

Positive and significant RE_VALUE coefficients in all specifications. Column 1 (simplest OLS) shows RE_VALUE coefficient of 0.0030 (1% significance): a $1 increase in real estate value increases cash dividends by 0.30 cents. Results hold across state-level residential prices, MSA-level residential prices, and city-level commercial prices, and in both OLS and IV specifications.

### B. Effect of Financial Flexibility on Share Repurchases

Positive and significant RE_VALUE coefficients when firm-level controls are included. An increase in financial flexibility leads to an increase in share repurchases.

### C. Payout Flexibility and Discretionary Payout

Positive and significant effect of RE_VALUE on payout flexibility (ratio of share repurchases to total payout), consistent with managers allocating more of the increases in debt capacity to share repurchases than to dividends, since shocks tend to be temporary. The effect of financial flexibility on discretionary payouts is more than twice that on nondiscretionary payouts.

### D. Leverage Effect during Periods of Decreasing Real Estate Prices

During the 2008–2011 real estate bust, highly leveraged firms cut dividends in response to decreasing financial flexibility. The triple interaction BUST_DUMMY × HIGH_LEVERAGE_DUMMY × RE_VALUE is positive and significant, indicating that highly leveraged firms that see their collateral values fall are significantly more likely to cut dividends.

### E. Availability of Investment Opportunities and Financial Constraints

The RE_VALUE coefficients are significantly higher for firms with fewer investment opportunities (low Tobin's Q) than for firms with more investment opportunities. Among firms with fewer investment opportunities, financially constrained firms (top 3 deciles of SA index) make greater use of the collateral channel than unconstrained firms.

## 6. Robustness Tests

Results are robust to:
1. Using only small firms in large MSAs (addresses reverse-causality of large firms' payouts affecting local real estate prices)
2. Using average payout over the subsequent 3 years as the dependent variable
3. Using only firms listed for at least 10 years (addresses location-choice endogeneity)
4. Controlling for remotely-located firms paying higher dividends due to lower shareholder monitoring
5. Addressing the concern that the collateral channel might only be used to finance investments, not payouts

## 7. Conclusions

This article provides causal evidence that an increase in financial flexibility, induced through the collateral channel, leads to more cash dividends, share repurchases, and payout flexibility. The results show that the effects are stronger for discretionary payouts than nondiscretionary payouts, and are concentrated in firms with few investment opportunities. Highly leveraged firms are more likely to cut their payouts during periods of sharp decrease in real estate prices.
