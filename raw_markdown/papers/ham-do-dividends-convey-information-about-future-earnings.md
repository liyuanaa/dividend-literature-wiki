# Do dividends convey information about future earnings?

- **Authors:** Ham, Charles G.; Kaplan, Zachary R.; Leary, Mark T.
- **Journal:** Journal of Financial Economics
- **Year:** 2020
- **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jfineco.2019.10.006

## Abstract

Yes. We show that dividend changes contain information about highly persistent changes in future economic income. Three methodological differences lead us to different conclusions from the extant literature: (i) we use an "event window approach" to cleanly delineate earnings after dividend changes from those before, (ii) we use alternative earnings measures to control for endogenous investment and asset write-downs surrounding dividend changes, and (iii) we control for the nonlinear relation between dividend changes and market reactions. Our results suggest dividend announcement returns reflect information about the level of permanent earnings, though the timing of the information content is difficult to reconcile with traditional signaling models.

## 1 Introduction

The strong positive market reaction to announcements of dividend changes is one of the most robust empirical findings in corporate finance. Yet, the forces behind this reaction are still not fully understood. Miller and Modigliani (1961) suggest that dividend changes may reveal managers' information about the firm's future earnings prospects to investors. However, the literature provides conflicting evidence on the nature and extent of the earnings information contained in dividend announcements.

In surveys (Lintner, 1956; Brav et al., 2005), managers say they view dividends as a persistent commitment to pay out cash and, therefore, change dividends only when anticipating a permanent change in earnings. Consistent with this intuition, several studies find that dividend changes are associated with greater persistence of past earnings changes (e.g., Benartzi et al., 1997; Guay and Harford, 2000; Koch and Sun, 2004) and that higher dividend levels are associated with higher and more persistent earnings (Fama and French, 1998; Skinner and Soltes, 2011). These findings would seem to suggest that dividend changes should be useful for predicting future unexpected earnings. However, the evidence on this question is decidedly mixed.

In this paper, we make several important corrections to the measurement of the information content of dividends that help reconcile these previous findings and shed new light on the nature of the cash flow news in dividend announcements. We show evidence that dividend changes do in fact convey information about future economic income that persists for at least three years, suggesting that dividend increasing (decreasing) firms move to a new and higher (lower) level of permanent earnings. In addition, the magnitude of the revealed information about unexpected earnings is large enough to explain the market reaction to dividend changes. However, we also find that much of the earnings information conveyed to the market through dividends would have been conveyed to the market through earnings in the first year after the dividend declaration, suggesting that managers may be unwilling to bear significant costs solely to accelerate this news into price.

We begin by showing that much of the disagreement about the relation between dividend changes and future earnings changes results from timing differences in the computation of future earnings changes. Many prior studies use a "fiscal year" approach, which groups dividends and earnings into fiscal years and compares earnings in the fiscal year containing the dividend change to earnings in the following fiscal year (and, in some cases, comparing earnings in fiscal year t + 1 to those in t + 2). This approach has two potential drawbacks: first, quarterly earnings announced after the dividend change but before the end of the fiscal year will be considered part of past or current (rather than future) earnings. Second, earnings changes realized soon after the dividend announcement will change the baseline against which future earnings are compared.

By contrast, we use quarterly data and an "event window" approach that compares earnings announced after the dividend change to earnings in the comparable period before the dividend change (see also Aharony and Dotan, 1994). This approach cleanly delineates past and future earnings reports, relative to the time of the dividend announcement. Because investors will update their expectation of future cash flows for all earnings announced after the dividend change, we argue the event window approach is more appropriate for measuring the information content of dividend announcements.

In our sample, the fiscal year approach produces no evidence of a relation between dividend changes and future unexpected earnings. However, using the "event window" approach, we show strong evidence that dividend changes predict future earnings changes. These results are robust to several proxies for expected earnings, including linear and nonlinear functions of past earnings levels, earnings changes, and stock returns (Grullon et al., 2005), a matched sample of non-changers (Benartzi et al., 1997), and analyst earnings forecasts. Further, these differences in future earnings are highly persistent: dividend changes help explain the level of unexpected earnings up to at least three years after the dividend change.

We make several contributions. First, we show that dividend changes provide information about persistent changes in future earnings, which differs from extant conclusions on dividend information content. However, our evidence is consistent with the broader literature linking the level of dividends to the level of expected earnings. Second, we explain the attenuation in information content noted in earlier studies as a function of the accounting system's tendency to incorporate investment and asset write-downs in earnings. We argue gross profit is better suited to studying the horizon of economic income associated with disclosure. Third, while both empirical and survey results suggest firms that increase their dividend implicitly commit to maintain the dividend, we show this assumption is less descriptive of extremely large increases.

## 2 Relation to prior literature

The information content hypothesis posits that investors react to dividend changes because the change in payout commitment conveys information about the earnings prospects of the firm (Miller and Modigliani, 1961). This view has several testable implications. First, if dividend decisions are a function of managers' private information about current and future earnings, dividend increases (decreases) should be followed by higher (lower) unexpected earnings realizations. Second, if investors recognize the earnings news reflected in dividend announcements, dividend changes should be greeted by price changes in the same direction. Related, investors should update their expectations about future earnings following announced dividend changes.

A lengthy literature tests whether dividends contain information about unexpected future earnings changes. While early studies produced mixed results, several showed positive evidence for the information content view (e.g., Ofer and Siegel, 1987; Carroll, 1995; Aharony and Dotan, 1994; Yoon and Starks, 1995; Nissim and Ziv, 2001). More recently, though, most large sample empirical studies argue dividend changes contain little or no information about future earnings (Watts, 1973; Gonedes, 1978; Penman, 1983; Lang and Litzenberger, 1989; DeAngelo et al., 1996; Benartzi et al., 1997; Grullon et al., 2002; Grullon et al., 2005).

## 3 Sample selection and descriptive statistics

We obtain data on dividend declarations from the Center for Research in Securities Prices (CRSP) events database. We first select all ordinary quarterly dividend declarations (distribution code 1232) over the period 1971–2016 for which the firm made a previous quarterly dividend declaration in the past 180 days. This allows us to compute the percentage dividend change. We limit the sample to: (i) firms listed on the NYSE, Amex, or Nasdaq exchanges, (ii) ordinary common stocks (i.e., those with share code 10 or 11), and (iii) nonfinancial firms (we exclude firms with a four-digit Standard Industrial Classification (SIC) code beginning with six). Our sample covers 165,558 observations for the one-year-ahead tests (1971–2016).
