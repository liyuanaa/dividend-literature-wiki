# Juicing the dividend yield: Mutual funds and the demand for dividends

- **Authors:** Harris, Lawrence E.; Hartzmark, Samuel M.; Solomon, David H.
- **Journal:** Journal of Financial Economics
- **Year:** 2015
- **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jfineco.2015.04.001

## Abstract

Some mutual funds purchase stocks before dividend payments to artificially increase their dividends, which we call "juicing." Funds paid more than twice the dividends implied by their holdings in 7.4% of fund-years examined. Juicing is associated with larger inflows, and is more common among funds with unsophisticated investors. This behavior is consistent with an underlying investor demand for dividends, but is hard to explain by taxes or need for income, as funds can generate equivalent tax-free distributions by returning capital. Juicing is costly to investors through higher turnover and increased taxes of 0.57% to 1.52% of fund assets per year.

## 1 Introduction

A large literature in finance examines whether managers of actively managed funds add value to their investors by generating abnormal returns (Jensen, 1968; Carhart, 1997; Fama and French, 2010; Berk and Green, 2004). This debate discusses the problem in terms of the largely unstated assumption that investors in these funds care only about high risk-adjusted returns. While investors surely care about returns, it is not so obvious that they only care about returns. Managers maximize their compensation by increasing the money they manage. If investors care about other mutual fund attributes, managers may provide these attributes to attract additional flows even if doing so lowers fund returns.

One such attribute that investors may desire is the payment of dividends. While Miller and Modigliani (1961) famously showed that firm dividend policy should be irrelevant to investors, various theories suggest that at least some groups of investors will demand dividend-paying assets. The dividend clientele theory hypothesizes that groups of investors demand dividends based on their tax status or desire for income streams. Dividend catering (Baker and Wurgler, 2004) posits an overall demand for dividends stemming from psychological or institutional reasons.

We examine investor demand for dividends in the context of equity mutual funds. We find that some funds trade in and out of dividend-paying stocks to increase their dividend yield, consistent with fund investors having a desire for dividends. We refer to this strategy as "juicing." This behavior is not well explained by either taxes or a need for income. Funds can generate identical income streams by making tax-free distributions of invested capital instead of acquiring and distributing taxable dividends.

We find that, among dividend-paying equity mutual funds, in 7.4% of fund-years, funds paid dividend distributions more than twice as large as their quarterly position reports imply. Using a bootstrap analysis, we show that discrepancies between actual and implied dividends are much larger than the effects of random trade timing (i.e., unrelated to dividend dates) can explain. Overall, 28.5% of fund-years among dividend-paying funds show juicing at a level greater than the median 1% significance cutoff from the distribution of bootstrapped ratios. In addition, funds that juice in one year are much more likely to juice in other years. This evidence indicates that paying more dividends than implied by holdings is a persistent property of such funds, consistent with it being a deliberate fund policy.

In addition, funds with high excess dividend ratios hold shares whose properties are consistent with a strategy designed to increase dividends. In particular, we examine the length of time from the report date of holdings until the next dividend paid by the stock. If funds are deliberately buying shares to increase dividends, at each point in time they should hold stocks that are closer to paying a dividend. Consistent with this view, when funds have a high excess dividend ratio the average time to next dividend for reported holdings is lower than for similar funds. Funds with an excess dividend ratio greater than 1.38 hold stocks that are on average 4.1 days closer to the next dividend payment, roughly 7.3% of the average time until next dividend payment.

Investors appear to reward fund managers who engage in juicing, as juicing is associated with significant capital inflows. Funds that have an excess dividend ratio greater than 1.38 receive on average an additional 6.8% in flows per year compared to other funds with similar observable characteristics, while an excess dividend ratio greater than 2.00 is associated with an additional 12.2% in flows per year.

If juicing appeals to investors with a psychological desire for dividends, we might expect these investors to be less sophisticated. Consistent with this expectation, juicing is significantly less likely for funds with an institutional share class, as institutions are often considered to be more informed. This result also militates against a tax clientele explanation. Dividends are generally taxed as income, albeit at reduced income tax rates since 2003 (when qualified), and thus tend to accrue higher tax liabilities than other payout methods such as repurchases. Under a clientele explanation, dividends should be more valuable to investors with lower income tax rates, or those who pay no income tax at all. By contrast, juicing is more likely among retail funds, whose investors are more likely to pay income tax on dividends.

Juicing is costly to investors in various ways. Trading in and out of stocks as part of a juicing strategy increases trading costs. We find that juicing funds have higher portfolio turnover than comparable non-juicing funds: by 11% per year for funds with excess dividend ratios above 1.38 and by 17% per year for funds with a dividend ratio above 2.00. Juicing also is costly from a tax perspective, as funds can generate equivalent income streams with fewer tax consequences for their investors by simply returning capital, which they rarely do. Dividend payments generally are taxed as ordinary income (though at lower rates if qualified), while returns of capital are not. We estimate that the annual additional tax payments that an investor would face in a fund with an excess dividend ratio greater than 2.00 are approximately 0.57% of the total fund assets per year during the period 2004–2012, and 1.52% of fund assets per year between 1993 and 2003.

Our results are consistent with investors who psychologically distinguish between consuming income produced by their assets and consuming the capital values of their assets. Note that the distinction concerns the labeling of a given payment as a dividend, and not the timing or amount of the payment itself. Thus, although returns of capital could more cheaply satisfy any need for regular cash payments, investors may not value returns of capital as much as dividend payments from juicing funds, because the former are not labeled as "income" produced by the asset.

Our results also help explain how the demands of many small individual investors can aggregate into significant market demands. For example, Hartzmark and Solomon (2013) find that demand for shares in the lead-up to dividend payments appears to increase, causing predictable price increases in dividend payment months. Our results suggest that juicing mutual funds aggregate these individual demands and actively trade on behalf of these investors.

## 2 Literature review

This study contributes to a large literature on why funds pay dividends. Most relevant to the current paper are studies that examine why the return on ex-dividend days is on average positive — that is, that the price change on the ex-dividend day averages less than the amount of the dividend (Campbell and Beranek 1955). Elton and Gruber (1970) argue that dividend clienteles cause this shortfall because the marginal investor pays taxes on dividends.

This study also relates to the literature on agency problems within mutual funds, including risk-shifting and the illegal behavior of trading funds after hours at stale Net Asset Values (Kisin, 2012). The current study also relates to the literature on how funds sometimes "game" their holdings, a phenomenon known as "window dressing."

## 3 Data

The data on mutual fund holdings are from Thomson Reuters, and are taken from the N-CSR, N-CSRS, and N-Q filings with the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC). The data on mutual fund returns, distributions, and other fund information come from the CRSP Mutual Funds database. The data on stock returns, prices, and dividends come from the CRSP stock database. Our analysis examines funds from 1990 through 2011, considering 2,224 funds with a total of 9,418 fund-year observations.
