# Dividend Policy at Firms Accused of Accounting Fraud

- **Authors:** Caskey, Judson; Hanlon, Michelle
- **Journal:** Contemporary Accounting Research
- **Year:** 2013
- **DOI:** 10.1111/j.1911-3846.2012.01173.x

## Abstract

Recent studies and some policy experts have posited that dividends indicate higher-quality earnings. In this study, we test this conjecture by comparing the dividend policies of firms accused of accounting fraud to those of firms not accused of accounting fraud. Specifically, we examine whether alleged fraud firms are as likely to be dividend payers as non-fraud firms, and whether managers of dividend-paying fraud firms increase dividends at the same rate as managers of non-fraud firms. Our data reveal that dividend paying status is negatively associated with the probability of committing accounting fraud. In addition, we also find that, during the alleged fraud period, the earnings-dividends relation is weaker for the alleged fraud firms relative to firms not accused of fraud. Finally, using propensity score match tests, the data provide evidence that managers of alleged fraud firms increase dividends less often than managers of firms not accused of fraud, consistent with the alleged fraud firms not being able to match the dividend policies of firms not accused of fraud. Overall, our results suggest that dividends, especially dividend increases, are associated with higher earnings quality.

Published in: Contemporary Accounting Research, Vol. 30, No. 2 (Summer 2013), pp. 818–850.

### Sample

- AAER (Accounting and Auditing Enforcement Release) firms: firms accused of fraud by the SEC, compared against (a) matched non-AAER firms and (b) all firms in the same industries.
- 72 dividend-paying AAER firms paid over $19.9 billion in ordinary cash dividends while perpetrating the alleged fraud (average 2.1% of pre-fraud market cap).

### Hypotheses

- H1: Dividend paying status is negatively associated with accounting fraud.
- H2: The relation between dividends and current earnings is weaker for fraud firms in years of earnings increases.
- H3: Managers of fraud firms increase dividends less often than managers of non-fraud firms.

### Key Findings

- Dividend paying status is negatively associated with the probability of accounting fraud (H1 confirmed): being a dividend payer reduces the probability of committing fraud.
- During the alleged fraud period, the earnings-dividends relation (Lintner/Fama-Babiak model) is significantly weaker for AAER firms than for non-AAER firms in years of earnings increases (H2 confirmed).
- Propensity score tests: managers of dividend-paying AAER firms are 11–17 percentage points less likely to increase dividends compared to managers of matched non-AAER firms (H3 confirmed). Fraudulent earnings cannot support the same rate of dividend increases per dollar of earnings.
- AAER firms have lower share repurchases than non-AAER firms in the year prior to fraud; no significant difference in repurchase changes during the fraud period.
- Equity issues and borrowing are associated with fraud, but have little impact on the dividend-fraud relation.

### Interpretation

Results support the view that dividends constrain fraudulent reporting: managers committing fraud know ex ante that overstated earnings cannot sustain established dividend policies, so either they avoid fraud (the constraining effect) or they fail to maintain the expected dividend-earnings relation. The 72 dividend-paying AAER firms demonstrate that dividends do not guarantee non-manipulation, but on average dividend-paying firms are significantly less likely to commit fraud.
