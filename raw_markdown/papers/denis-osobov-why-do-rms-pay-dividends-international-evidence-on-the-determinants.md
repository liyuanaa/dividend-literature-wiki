# Why do firms pay dividends? International evidence on the determinants of dividend policy

- **Authors:** Denis, David J.; Osobov, Igor
- **Journal:** Journal of Financial Economics
- **Year:** 2008
- **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jfineco.2007.06.006

## Abstract

In the US, Canada, UK, Germany, France, and Japan, the propensity to pay dividends is higher among larger, more profitable firms, and those for which retained earnings comprise a large fraction of total equity. Although there are hints of reductions in the propensity to pay dividends in most of the sample countries over the 1994–2002 period, they are driven by a failure of newly listed firms to initiate dividends when expected to do so. Dividend abandonment and the failure to initiate by existing nonpayers are economically unimportant except in Japan. Moreover, in each country, aggregate dividends have not declined and are concentrated among the largest, most profitable firms. Finally, outside of the US there is little evidence of a systematic positive relation between relative prices of dividend-paying and non-paying firms and the propensity to pay dividends. Overall, these findings cast doubt on signaling, clientele, and catering explanations for dividends, but support agency cost-based lifecycle theories.

## 1. Introduction

Since the publication of the original Miller and Modigliani (1961) irrelevance propositions, the question of why some firms pay dividends while others do not has puzzled financial economists. This paper extends the literature by examining cross-sectional and time-series evidence on the propensity to pay dividends in six developed financial markets (US, Canada, UK, Germany, France, and Japan) over the period 1989–2002. The paper tests whether firm characteristics, time trends, and catering explanations can account for cross-country patterns.

DeAngelo, DeAngelo, and Stulz (2006) propose a life-cycle theory that combines Jensen (1986) agency theory with evolution in the firm's investment opportunity set. This theory predicts that in early years, firms pay few dividends because investment opportunities exceed internally generated capital; in later years, internal funds exceed investment opportunities and firms optimally pay out excess funds to mitigate the possibility that free cash flows would be wasted.

## 2. Sample

Constructed using Worldscope data from Thomson One Banker Analytics, 1989–2002. Sample includes US, Canada, UK, Germany, France, and Japan. Utilities (SIC 4900–4949), financial firms (SIC 6000–6999), and firms with negative book equity excluded.

## 3. Determinants of the Propensity to Pay Dividends

**Univariate analysis (Table 1):** In all six countries, dividend payers tend to be larger, more profitable, and have substantially higher ratios of retained earnings to total equity (RE/BE) than non-payers.

**Earned/contributed equity (Table 2):** The proportion of payers is near-zero when RE/BE is negative, rising to above 0.85 in all countries when RE/BE exceeds 0.90. A strong, nearly monotonic relationship between the earned/contributed equity mix and the proportion of dividend payers.

**Logit regressions (Table 4):** The likelihood of paying dividends is positively related to firm size, profitability, and the RE/BE mix in all six countries. The effect of growth opportunities is more mixed across countries. Path dependence in dividend policies is evident: firms currently paying dividends prefer continuing to do so; non-payers prefer not to initiate.

## 4. International Evidence on Changes in Propensity to Pay

**Baseline estimates (Table 5):** The expected percentage of payers declines over 1994–2002 in all countries except Japan, with magnitude ranging from 15.3% (France) to 21.9% (Germany). The actual declines, measured as expected minus actual, grow over time.

**New lists vs. abandonment (Table 8):** The shortfall in dividend payers in 2002 is driven primarily by failure of post-1993 entrants to initiate dividends (accounting for more than 80% of the shortfall in each country except Japan). Dividend abandonment by existing payers is economically unimportant except in Japan, where financial troubles during the 1990s economic slowdown appear to drive abandonment.

**Constant composition sample (Table 7, Panel B):** Median annual changes in the propensity to pay dividends are only 1.8%, 3.6%, and 1.9% in the US, Germany, and France respectively when restricted to firms present in 1989–1993. No systematic decline in Canada or UK.

## 5. Concentration of Dividends and Earnings

**Aggregate dividends (Table 9):** Total real dividends increase between 1990 and 2002 in all countries except Canada.

**Concentration (Table 10):** The top 20% of dividend payers account for at least 73.3% of aggregate dividends in all subperiods and all countries. In recent years, dividend payers account for more than 90% of aggregate market capitalization in all countries except the US and Canada.

## 6. Catering Hypothesis Tests

Outside the US, little evidence exists that the propensity to pay dividends or time-series changes in that propensity can be explained by changes in investor sentiment toward dividend-paying stocks (the Baker and Wurgler [2004a,b] dividend premium). Individual firms do not start and stop dividend payments in response to the market's relative valuation of dividend-paying firms.

## 7. Conclusion

The empirical determinants of dividends are remarkably similar across six developed countries. The concentration of dividends and earnings among the largest, most profitable firms casts doubt on signaling and clientele theories. The patterns are consistent with the life-cycle theory's central prediction that the distribution of free cash flow is the primary determinant of dividend policy. Propensity declines, where observed, are driven by the failure of newly listed firms to initiate dividends.
