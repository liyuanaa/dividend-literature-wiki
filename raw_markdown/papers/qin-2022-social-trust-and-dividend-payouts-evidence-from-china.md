# Social Trust and Dividend Payouts: Evidence from China

- **Authors:** Qin, Wei; Liang, Quanxi; Jiao, Yan; Lu, Meiting; Shan, Yaowen
- **Journal:** Pacific-Basin Finance Journal
- **Year:** 2022
- **DOI:** 10.1016/j.pacfin.2022.101726

## Abstract

Social trust helps to reduce information asymmetry and agency conflicts between insiders and investors, and thus alleviates the pressure on firms to pay cash dividends. Consistent with this view, we find that Chinese firms in low-trust regions make higher dividend payouts than those in high-trust regions. The negative effect of trust is more pronounced in firms with lower information transparency and weaker corporate governance. We additionally find a lower market reaction to dividend announcements for firms in high-trust areas, and moreover that the role of social trust is more important for firms that are lacking in political connections and located in areas with weak institutions. Our results are robust to alternative measures of social trust and dividends and the use of the instrumental variable approach to alleviate endogeneity concerns. Overall, the findings highlight the important role of social trust in corporate governance and corporate dividend policy, along with its ability to substitute for formal institutions.

### 1. Introduction

Social trust, as an informal institution, reduces market transaction costs, promotes interpersonal cooperation, and mitigates agency conflicts. The paper examines the role of regional social trust in shaping corporate dividend policy for Chinese listed firms.

Two competing theoretical channels:
- **Positive trust → higher dividends (H1b):** High trust reduces cost of capital, improves access to external capital, and managers are more ethical — less incentive to hoard cash.
- **Negative trust → lower dividends (H1a — supported):** Low-trust environments exacerbate agency conflicts and information asymmetry; shareholders demand higher dividends as an alternative governance mechanism; signaling role of dividends is more important when information quality is low.

The paper finds support for H1a in China. This contrasts with Hasan and Habib (2020) and Hossain et al. (2021) finding a positive trust–dividend relation in the U.S. (where formal institutions are strong). Together, these findings support La Porta et al.'s (2000) two agency models of dividends operating in different institutional environments.

### 3. Research Design

**Sample:** Chinese A-share listed firms, 2008–2018. Excludes financial firms, ST firms, and firms with missing data. Final sample: 24,086 firm-year observations.

**Dividend measures:**
- Dividend1 = cash dividends / total assets
- Dividend2 = cash dividends / net profits (payout ratio)
- Robustness: Dividend3 (/ sales), Dividend4 (/ operating income), Dividend5 (/ net assets)

**Social trust measure (primary):** Province-level index from China General Social Survey (CGSS), Question A33 ("Do you agree that the vast majority of people in this society can be trusted?"). Calculated as proportion of "strongly agree" + "somewhat agree" responses. Mean Trust = 0.607 across provinces.

Alternative trust measures: TrustHE (highly educated respondents), TrustHC (highly socially cognitive respondents), TrustScore (weighted average), TrustHS (strongly agree only), TrustDonors (voluntary blood donation rate), TrustInterest (inverse of 1930s grain lending interest rates), TrustNGO (NGO proportion).

**Control variables:** PerGDP, Market (marketization index), Religion, Size, Lev, ROE, Tangibility, FCF, Age, BoardSize, BoardInd. Industry and year fixed effects.

### 4. Results

**Baseline (Table 3):**
- Dividend1: coefficient on Trust = −0.081 (t = −4.66***)
- Dividend2: coefficient on Trust = −0.090 (t = −3.72***)
- Univariate comparison: firms in low-trust regions pay 0.018 higher Dividend1 and 0.016 higher Dividend2 (both significant at 1% level).

**Instrumental variable (Table 4):**
Instruments: (1) historical number of wars in province over past 4,000 years (War); (2) number of distinct dialects in province (Dialects). Both satisfy relevance (F-statistics >> 10) and plausible exclusion. IV estimates confirm negative and significant coefficient on Trust for both dividend measures.

**Robustness (Table 5):** Results hold across all alternative dividend and trust measures; hold after controlling for additional macro variables, family control, controlling shareholder ownership concentration, and alternative sample selections.

### 5. Cross-Sectional Tests

**Information transparency moderates the effect:**
- Effect stronger for firms with lower information transparency (e.g., lower analyst coverage, non-Big-4 auditor, lower earnings quality).
- Consistent with social trust serving as a substitute for formal information quality in reducing investor uncertainty.

**Corporate governance moderates the effect:**
- Effect stronger for firms with weaker internal governance (lower board independence, no large institutional blockholder).
- Social trust as an informal governance substitute.

**Institutional environment:**
- Effect stronger in regions with lower degree of marketization (Market index).
- Effect stronger in regions with less effective legal system.
- Consistent with social trust substituting for formal legal/market institutions.

**Political connections:**
- Effect more pronounced for non-politically connected firms.
- Politically connected firms have alternative governance channels; social trust is relatively more important for firms without political access.

**Market reactions:**
- Lower market reaction to dividend announcements for firms in high-trust areas, consistent with dividends carrying less informational content when trust is high.

### 6. Conclusion

Regional social trust in China acts as an informal institution that substitutes for formal governance and investor protection mechanisms. Firms in low-trust regions face greater agency conflicts and information asymmetry, leading shareholders to demand higher cash dividends. The effect is amplified by weak information transparency, weak corporate governance, weak legal institutions, and absence of political connections. The contrast with U.S. findings confirms that the direction of the trust–dividend relationship depends on the quality of formal institutions in the operating environment.
