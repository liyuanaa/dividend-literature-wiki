# Taxation and Dividend Policy: The Muting Effect of Agency Issues and Shareholder Conflicts

- **Authors:** Jacob, Martin; Michaely, Roni
- **Journal:** The Review of Financial Studies
- **Year:** 2017
- **DOI:** 10.1093/rfs/hhx041

## Abstract

Using proprietary data on the entire spectrum of ownership structure and exact tax status of investors and firms, we examine how dividend taxation affects payout. Utilizing an exogenous shock to dividend taxation, we show that absent any frictions, dividend taxation has a large impact on payout. As agency issues and shareholder conflicts increase, owners' tax preferences have significantly smaller impact on payout. Three mechanisms reduce the dividend-tax sensitivity: Coordination among owners, heterogeneity in tax preferences, and diverging objectives between managers and owners. Altogether, taxation has a first-order impact on payout, but agency issues and shareholder conflicts mute its impact substantially. (JEL G30, G35, H24, H25)

## 1. Introduction

Policy makers frequently use changes in capital taxation to affect dividend payout. Using a large dividend tax cut, we examine the dividend tax responsiveness and how it is affected by other frictions, such as agency issues and conflicting objectives among shareholders. Unlike prior studies, we use comprehensive linked firm-owner data with information on the actual investors' tax rates for practically every owner, and information on the exact ownership structure of a large panel of all closely held corporations (CHCs) in Sweden.

Our setting with these small, entrepreneurial firms has three important advantages over prior studies that enable us to shed new light on the effect of taxes on dividend policy and how this tax effect interacts with other frictions. First, while, by necessity, all prior studies had to rely on proxies for investors' tax rates, we were able to obtain, for the first time, complete and exact data on the tax status of each owner and firm in our sample. Second, we isolate the effect of taxes on dividend policy in an environment that is almost void of frictions that might reduce tax sensitivity. Third, our data enable us to shed light on the dividend tax sensitivity of small, private firms, an important part of the economy.

Our first finding is that, absent any frictions, taxes have a large impact on dividend policy. Second, we show that conflicts of interest between owners and management result in the weaker response of dividends to tax changes. We find that this is not only friction that interacts with the effect of taxes on dividends. Shareholder conflicts, such as owner heterogeneity in tax preferences or coordination issues among owners, also explain why dividend tax responsiveness declines as ownership becomes more dispersed. Our third main finding is that the owners of small, entrepreneurial firms trade off dividend and labor income taxes in payout decisions.

## 2. Institutional Background and the Taxation of Corporations in Sweden

Sweden has a classical corporate tax system with a double taxation of dividends, with corporate tax and dividend taxation, as many other countries. About 315,000 corporations are registered in Sweden that are subject to corporate taxes. A non-listed corporation is closely held for tax purposes if four or fewer shareholders possess at least 50% of the voting rights and if at least one shareholder is active in the operation of the firm.

The 2006 tax reform contained two changes concerning dividend taxation. First, the combined dividend tax rate was reduced from 49.6% prior to 2006 to 42.4% after the reform. Second, the dividend allowance — the amount of dividends that can be paid out at this reduced dividend tax rate, rather than the higher income tax rate — more than doubled.

## 3. Tax Sensitivity of Dividends

Using the 2006 tax reform as an exogenous event and a difference-in-difference (DD) approach, we find that highly taxed owner-managers increased their dividend compensation ratio by about 5.9 percentage points (a 44% increase relative to the pre-reform average) following the reform.

The DDD analysis shows that owners of wholly owned firms with tax preferences for dividends increased the percentage of compensation paid as dividends by about 7.3 percentage points (69% increase relative to pre-reform average). This tax sensitivity gradually decreases as agency issues and shareholder conflicts become more likely to be present. Owners in firms with three owners increase the dividend compensation ratio by only 4.4 percentage points — about 40% less than the effect for firms with one owner. Once a firm has five owners, the individual owners' tax preferences have very little impact, if any, on the firm's payout policy.

## 4. Mechanisms

Three mechanisms reduce dividend-tax sensitivity:

1. **Coordination costs among shareholders**: Firms with only one owner show economically significant substitution between dividends and wages. The propensity to substitute wages with dividends decreases by 39% for firms with three owners relative to wholly owned firms.

2. **Heterogeneity in tax preferences**: In firms with greater tax heterogeneity across owners, the response to the reform is significantly weaker than for similar firms with more homogeneous tax preferences, even if, on average, owners prefer dividend payouts.

3. **Agency conflicts between managers and owners**: Dividend compensation is less responsive to taxation when there is separation of ownership and control.

## 5. Data and Sample

The study uses the Firm Register and Individual Database (FRIDA), provided by Statistics Sweden. The data contain the corporate tax returns of all Swedish corporations over the period 2000–2009. The final sample contains 1,172,617 observations for the owner-managers of CHCs from 134,130 firms and 247,861 owner-managers.

Summary statistics show that, on average, 70% of total compensation from the CHC is in the form of wages, while 16% is in the form of dividends. Average firm has two active owners, holds 26% of its assets as cash, has a ratio of retained earnings (debt) to assets of about 24% (63%), and has about six employees.

## 6. Conclusions

This paper shows that taxation has a first-order impact on payout decisions when there are no frictions. However, agency issues and shareholder conflicts mute the impact of taxation substantially. The owners of closely held corporations appear to respond more strongly to the 2006 tax reform than owners of CHCs with more diffuse ownership, even if they have a preference for dividends. These findings suggest that theoretical and empirical work examining the impact of taxation on payout policy might be incomplete without considering the interaction of other frictions with firm dividend policy.
