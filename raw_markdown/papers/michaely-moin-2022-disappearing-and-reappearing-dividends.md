# Disappearing and Reappearing Dividends

- **Authors:** Michaely, Roni; Moin, Amani
- **Journal:** Journal of Financial Economics
- **Year:** 2022
- **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jfineco.2021.06.029

## Abstract

We decompose the decrease (1970s–2000) and subsequent recovery (2000–2018) in the fraction of dividend-paying firms. Changes in firm characteristics and proclivity to pay (probability of paying dividends conditional on characteristics) each drive half of the dividend disappearance. A higher proclivity drives 82% of the dividend reappearance. The remaining 18% is driven by a single characteristic: reduced earnings volatility. Changing characteristics are associated with low-profitability, high-earnings-volatility firms. Changing proclivity is associated with stable, profitable firms. Rather than dividend initiations or omissions, newly listed and delisted firms drive trends. Finally, the magnitude and duration of disappearing total payout is substantially smaller than that of dividends, indicating some substitution between dividends and repurchases.

## Journal of Financial Economics 143 (2022) 207–226

**JEL Classification:** G35, G32

**Keywords:** Dividends; Payout policy; Stock repurchases

### 1. Introduction

Fama and French (2001) showed a startling decline in the fraction of firms paying dividends (disappearing dividends). Grullon and Michaely (2002) showed a systemic substitution of repurchases for dividends. This paper extends and complements these studies by showing that the fraction of dividend-paying firms begins to increase around 2000 ("reappearing dividends").

The central question is why the fraction of dividend-paying firms falls from 73% in 1978 to 23% in 2000, then increases to 36% in 2018.

### 2. Data

CRSP/Compustat merged fundamentals annual database, 1963–2018. Firms on major U.S. stock exchanges. Excludes: observations with missing data, assets < $500,000, book equity < $250,000, utilities (SIC 4900–4949), financial firms (SIC 6000–6999). Only common shares (CRSP code 10 or 11). Final sample: 153,544 firm-year observations, 14,489 unique firms.

A firm is a dividend payer if dividends per share > 0 in the fiscal year. Gross repurchases = total expenditure on purchase of common and preferred stocks minus reduction in redemption value of preferred stocks.

### 3. Firm Characteristics and Proclivity to Pay Dividends

**Logit model (Table 2):** Fama-MacBeth regressions of dividend payment on profitability, earnings volatility, NYSE percentile, Tobin's Q, and asset growth. Coefficients estimated for 1967–1977, then used to project expected payer fraction 1978–2018.

Key coefficient magnitudes (odds ratios):
- One SD increase in profitability → 2.8× probability of paying dividends.
- One SD increase in earnings volatility → 11.4× probability of not paying dividends.
- One SD increase in size → 3.1× probability of paying dividends.

**Disappearing dividends (1978–2000):**
- Total decline: 49.31 pp.
- Attributable to changing firm characteristics: 52.62%.
- Attributable to changing proclivity: 47.38%.
- Characteristics channel driven mainly by influx of low-profitability, high-earnings-volatility firms.

**Reappearing dividends (2000–2018):**
- Total recovery: 12.62 pp.
- Attributable to changing proclivity: 81.72%.
- Attributable to changing characteristics: 18.28% (entirely due to reduced earnings volatility).
- Most other characteristics (profitability, Tobin's Q) continued to move in the direction opposing reappearance.

### 4. Model Specification and Market Structure

**Dividend persistence (Section 4.1):**
Adding lagged dividend payer status to model eliminates systematic prediction errors, confirming Lintner (1956) smoothing. Changes in dividend policy driven by firms entering/exiting the sample, not within-firm changes.

**Generation gap:**
- Firms with IPOs before 1978: stable fraction of payers ~69%, no trend.
- Firms with IPOs in 1978 or later: persistently lower proclivity (~19.5% actual payers vs. expected ~39%), driving both disappearance and reappearance trends.

**SEC rule 10b-18 and repurchase substitution (Section 4.2):**
After 1982, repurchases became legally easier. The gap between actual and expected *total payout* (dividends + repurchases) is much smaller (-9.1 pp trough in 1994) and shorter-lived (gap closes by 1998) than for dividends alone (-21.7 pp trough in 2002). This is consistent with firms substituting repurchases for dividends during the disappearing-dividends period. However, repurchases continue to increase even as dividends reappear post-2000.

**Delisting and reappearing dividends (Section 4.3):**
The absolute number of dividend-paying firms stays roughly constant (~820) from 2000 to 2018. Reappearance is driven primarily by non-dividend-paying firms delisting (mostly via mergers/acquisitions). A synthetic sample where no M&A delistings occur post-2000 shows dividends reappear to only one-third the actual extent.

### 5. Conclusion

Disappearing dividends are driven roughly equally by changing characteristics (new firms with low profitability and high earnings volatility) and changing proclivity (stable, profitable firms choosing not to pay). Reappearing dividends are driven primarily (~82%) by changing proclivity among existing firms. The single characteristic that contributes materially to reappearance is reduced earnings volatility. The total payout (dividends + repurchases) pattern is much less extreme, indicating substantial substitution between the two payout forms. Modern firms that do pay out are more likely to do so via repurchases alongside or instead of dividends compared to the pre-1978 era.
