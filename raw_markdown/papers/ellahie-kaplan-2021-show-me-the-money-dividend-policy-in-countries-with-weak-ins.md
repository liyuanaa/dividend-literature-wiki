# Show Me the Money! Dividend Policy in Countries with Weak Institutions

- **Authors:** Ellahie, Atif; Kaplan, Zachary
- **Journal:** Journal of Accounting Research
- **Year:** 2021
- **DOI:** 10.1111/1475-679X.12363

## Abstract

We hypothesize that, in weak-institution countries, firms adjust the "timing" of dividend payments by committing to distribute a percentage of current earnings as dividends, revealing the extent of firm-level agency conflicts to future investors and facilitating the raising of external capital. Consistent with this hypothesis, we find that, on average, firms in weak-institution countries have a higher speed of adjustment (SOA) to their target payout ratio, pay dividends earlier in the lifecycle, and are more likely to disclose a dividend policy committing to pay a minimum percentage of earnings. Within-country tests show that, in weak-institution countries, the firms with the highest SOA dividend policies have fewer agency problems and an increased ability to raise external capital. Finally, returns tests around earnings announcements show that high-SOA dividend policies are associated with larger market reactions to earnings in weak-institution countries. Collectively, our findings suggest that dividend policy helps to alleviate agency conflicts in weak-institution countries between firms and (future) investors.

## 1. Introduction

Information and agency problems between current shareholders and new investors can create a wedge between the cost of internal and external finance. Weak institutions at the country level exacerbate these firm-level agency problems and are associated with greater information frictions and costlier external finance. We hypothesize that firms with less severe agency conflicts in weak-institution countries balance competing forces by altering the timing of dividends relative to the smooth dividends observed in strong-institution countries.

Specifically, they commit to return a proportion of current earnings as dividends, enabling them to separate themselves from firms with more severe agency conflicts. This focus on timing of dividend payout differs from much of the prior literature on international dividends, which documents a lower level of dividend payout in weak-institution countries.

## 2. Hypotheses

H1: In countries with low institutional quality, firms will adjust dividends more rapidly in response to earnings changes and commit to do so through disclosure.

H2: Firms intending to raise equity capital will be more likely to adjust dividends rapidly in response to earnings changes in countries with weak as opposed to strong institutions.

## 3. Sample

~33,000 firms from 85 countries, FactSet Fundamentals Annual international database, 1987–2016. Firms must pay dividends in at least half of all years subsequent to the first dividend payment. SOA estimated following Leary and Michaely (2011). Institutional Quality from World Bank Worldwide Governance Indicators (government effectiveness, regulatory quality, rule of law, and control of corruption), normalized 0–1.

## 4. Results

**Country-level SOA and Institutional Quality (Table 2):** Institutional Quality coefficient = −0.42 (t = −7.61) in the 44-country baseline (R² = 48.2%). In weak-institution countries, earnings changes have a 42% larger effect on dividend changes than in the strongest institution country. Effect robust across all 85 countries and using Gross Payout SOA.

**Firm-level cross-sectional analysis (Table 3):** Institutional Quality coefficient of −0.39 (t = −5.44) in baseline; −0.33 (t = −4.79) after firm-level controls. One standard deviation change in Institutional Quality (0.22) associated with −0.086 change in SOA, large relative to SOA standard deviation (0.157). Robust to all six additional country-level characteristics (mandatory dividend rules, ownership structure, taxes, per capita GDP).

**Within-country firm heterogeneity (Table 4):** In weak-institution countries, high-SOA firms have higher market values (less agency conflict), stronger analyst following, higher audit fees (complementarity), lower return volatility, and greater equity issuance ability. Cross-listed firms have weaker SOA-Institutional Quality association, consistent with bonding to stronger institutions as an alternative mechanism.

**Stated dividend policies (Table 5):** 18% of 349 surveyed firms expressly commit to a minimum payout ratio (average minimum = 37%). Firms with minimum payout policies are located in weaker-institution countries and exhibit higher SOA.

**Life-cycle and equity issuance (Table 6–7):** Firms in weak-institution countries pay dividends earlier in their lifecycle and more often when growth options are high. High-SOA firms in weak-institution countries are more successful at equity issuance (larger amounts, better announcement returns).

**Earnings response coefficients:** High-SOA dividend policies are associated with larger market reactions to earnings in weak-institution countries.

## 5. Conclusion

In countries with weak institutions, firms use the timing of dividend payments — adjusting dividends rapidly in response to current earnings — as a commitment device to reveal low agency conflict and facilitate access to equity capital. This contrasts with strong-institution countries where smooth, life-cycle-based dividends are the norm. The mechanism operates through the reduction of information asymmetry between current shareholders and future investors, not through changes in the level of dividends.
