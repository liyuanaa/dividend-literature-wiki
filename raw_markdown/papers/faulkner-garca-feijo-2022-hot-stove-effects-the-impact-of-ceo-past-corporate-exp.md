# Hot-Stove Effects: The Impact of CEO Past Corporate Experiences on Dividend Policy

- **Authors:** Faulkner, Matthew; García-Feijóo, Luis
- **Journal:** Journal of Financial and Quantitative Analysis
- **Year:** 2022
- **DOI:** 10.1017/S002210902100034X

## Abstract

The personal traits of chief executive officers (CEOs) have been found to influence corporate policy decisions. We examine the impact of CEO past corporate distress experiences on payout policy. CEOs who have experienced a distress event in their career, while working in a non-CEO position at a different firm, subsequently alter corporate payout policy once in the CEO position. They are less likely to pay dividends and repurchase shares, pay out lower levels of dividends, and are less likely to increase dividends. They further exhibit preference toward repurchases. Overall, we report that experience-driven conservatism affects payout policy, a novel finding in the literature.

## 1. Introduction

Payout policy is one of the most important corporate decisions, but despite several decades of research, its determinants are not well understood. The corporate finance literature has found that CEO personal characteristics, attributes, and preferences influence corporate policies, but little attention has been given to the possible impact of a CEO's past corporate experiences on payout policy.

The psychological concept of the "hot-stove effect" (Denrell and March, 2001) justifies conjecture that past experiences alter payout policy. A person who has experienced a poor outcome will attempt to avoid similar outcomes in the future — in this context, CEOs making decisions that are more cautious and implementing more conservative payout policies.

## 2. Prior Literature

Dittmar and Duchin (2016) find that CEOs who experienced corporate financial distress in their prior career hold more cash, use less leverage, and decrease R&D spending at subsequent firms. Schoar and Zuo (2017) find that managers who begin their careers during recessions tend to be more conservative. The growing body of behavioral corporate finance research has few studies on managerial biases' impact on payout policy.

## 3. Hypotheses

H1: CEO past corporate distress experiences will decrease the likelihood of paying a dividend.
H2: CEOs with past corporate distress experiences will pay lower levels of dividends.
H3: CEO past corporate distress experiences will decrease the propensity to repurchase shares.
H4: The propensity to pay dividends or repurchase shares will be lower by firms whose CEOs have past corporate distress experiences.
H5a: CEOs with past corporate distress experiences will be less likely to increase a dividend.
H5b: CEOs with past corporate distress experiences will be more likely to decrease a dividend.
H6: Conditional on payout, the substitution of repurchases for dividends will be stronger for CEOs that have past corporate distress experiences.

## 4. Data and Variables

**Sample:** BoardEx and ExecuComp databases combined with CRSP and Compustat. ~4,228 firms, 1983–2017, ~37,000 firm-year observations. Financials (SIC 6000–6999) and utilities (SIC 4900–4949) excluded.

**Distress measures:** (i) bond rating downgrade; (ii) OCF shock (bottom decile of change in operating cash flow, conditional on negative change); (iii) stock price shock (bottom decile of annual stock return change, conditional on negative); (iv) dividend cut (total dividend payment decrease). DISTRESS_EXPERIENCES = 1 if CEO worked in a non-CEO role at a firm experiencing any of these. Approximately 27% of CEO-years have experienced past corporate distress.

**Endogeneity controls:** Distress events only counted from non-CEO roles at different firms.

## 5. Results

**Dividend propensity (Table 4):** DISTRESS_EXPERIENCES coefficient = −0.205 (p < 0.01), implying CEOs with prior distress are 18.5% less likely to pay a dividend. Effect significant across all four individual distress measures (OCF shock shows largest: 38.9% less likely; dividend distress: 9.5% less likely).

**Dividend levels (Table 5):** Composite distress measure coefficient = −0.164 (p < 0.01) on DIV_ASSETS. Predicted payout ratio lower by approximately 12% (composite) and 42% (OCF shock) of the mean dividend-to-assets ratio.

**Repurchases and total payout (Table 6):** Propensity to repurchase 7.5% lower (REPURCHASE coefficient = −0.078, p < 0.01). Propensity to use any payout 12.8% lower (PAYOUT coefficient = −0.137, p < 0.01).

**Dividend changes (Table 6):** CEOs with distress experience are 11.7% less likely to increase dividends (DIV_INCREASE coefficient = −0.124, p < 0.01). Weak evidence of higher likelihood of cutting dividends (DIV_DECREASE coefficient = 0.091, p < 0.10).

**Substitution (Table 6):** Conditional on payout, CEOs with distress experience are 12.1% more likely to use repurchases than dividends (PAYOUT_STYLE coefficient = −0.129, p < 0.05).

**Endogeneity tests (Tables 7–8):** PSM and exogenous turnover samples confirm main results (DISTRESS_EXPERIENCES coefficients −0.289 and −0.209 for dividend propensity, respectively).

## 6. Additional Tests

Effects are larger for more distant (early-career) distress experiences, for multiple distress experiences, for top-5 executive role experiences, and when distress was followed by bankruptcy. Positive corporate experiences do not affect dividend policy, confirming the asymmetric pessimism bias (negative events have larger impact than positive counterparts).

## 7. Conclusion

CEO experience-driven conservatism is a novel determinant of payout policy. CEOs who have experienced corporate distress implement systematically more conservative dividend policies and prefer the flexibility of share repurchases over the long-term cash outflow commitment of dividends.
