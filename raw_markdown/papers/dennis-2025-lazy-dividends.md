# Lazy dividends

- **Authors:** Dennis, Patrick J.; Weston, James P.
- **Journal:** Journal of Corporate Finance
- **Year:** 2025
- **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jcorpfin.2025.102858

## Abstract

Dividends are clustered in increments of 5, such as 25, 50, and 75. Firms that gravitate towards these "prominent" amounts have lower operating performance and lower annual five-factor alphas of 77 b.p. Consistent with agency frictions that lead to lazy decisions, clustering effects are stronger for entrenched firms, with more market power, and less shareholder activism. Dividend increases also cluster more than cuts, consistent with saliency bias. In a counterfactual exercise, we find no similar patterns in a sample of ADRs. Our results complement a number of recent studies showing the economic importance of simple decision heuristics.

## 1. Introduction

Corporate dividends tend to cluster in amounts such as 25, 50, 75, etc., termed "prominent" numbers. This pattern is pervasive and persistent, with roughly a quarter of all dividends rounded to an amount ending in the digit "5" in 2018. The term "lazy" is used for these dividends because judgments that land on prominent numbers tend to signal low effort, resulting in suboptimal outcomes (Converse and Dennis, 2018). Prior work documents clustering in stock prices (Harris, 1991), trade sizes, limit orders, analyst earnings forecasts, and earnings realizations. This paper tests whether dividend clustering matters economically.

## 2. Data and Methodology

Sample covers 1Q1963 to 4Q2018 using CRSP quarterly dividend data for ordinary common shares (shrcd 10 or 11). A dividend is classified as prominent if the least-significant digit is "0" or "5." For performance analysis, firms must have issued a minimum of seven dividends during the 821-day window (365×2+91). Alphas computed relative to the Fama and French (2015) five-factor model for 12-, 24-, and 36-month horizons following the ex-dividend date. ROA measures: net income / assets; EBITDA (top-down and bottom-up definitions), adjusted by subtracting the median ROA for each industry-quarter. Governance proxies include institutional ownership (13F filings), outside director fraction (ISS), and poison pill provisions (ISS).

## 3. Firm Performance — Alpha (Tables 3, 4, 5)

12-month five-factor alpha ranges from 17.2 b.p. for low-prominent dividend payers to 11.8 b.p. for high-prominent payers. Non-prominent dividend payers have a 12-month alpha 6.4 b.p. higher than prominent payers (significant at 10%); 24- and 36-month alphas are 6.7 and 6.4 bp higher (significant at 5%). Matched-sample results (Table 5): non-prominent payers exceed prominent payers by 27.03 b.p. (at least 20 dividends) or 23.15 b.p. (at least 40 dividends).

Firms issuing non-prominent dividends also have significantly higher industry-adjusted Tobin's q (coefficient 0.03, significant at 5%). ADR placebo tests find no significant performance differences, consistent with the interpretation that clustering in U.S. domestic dividends reflects genuine managerial choice on round numbers.

## 4. Firm Performance — ROA (Table 6)

Companies issuing prominent dividends have ROA 0.7%–1.3% lower per year (significant at 1%). Results are consistent across three ROA definitions. ADR sample shows no significant effect.

## 5. Managerial Effort and Dividend Prominence

**Industry dominance (Table 8):** Dominant firms (highest revenue within 2-digit SIC-quarter) issue ~5–6% more prominent dividends than non-dominant peers, consistent with the quiet-life hypothesis (Hicks, 1935; Bertrand and Mullainathan, 2003).

**Governance (Table 9):** Institutional ownership and outside director fraction are both negatively related to the fraction of prominent dividends. Poison pill provision has a positive but insignificant coefficient.

**Salience (Table 9):** Dividend cuts are not significantly less prominent. Dividend initiations are significantly more prominent (+6 percentage points, at 1% level), consistent with initiations being "open" judgments lacking a prior anchor.

Special dividends (explicitly flagged and algorithm-identified) have substantially lower prominence rates (35.2% and 23.2% respectively) versus ordinary dividends (39.4%), supporting the hypothesis that more deliberate payout decisions yield fewer prominent amounts.

## 6. Dividend Changes (Table 12)

Dividend changes also cluster on prominent numbers. Managers who declare prominent levels tend to select prominent changes (25% prominent-level/prominent-change cell vs. 48% non-prominent/non-prominent cell). Conditional on issuing a prominent dividend, bad times elicit a greater reduction in prominent vs. non-prominent changes.

## 7. Time Trends

Strong negative time-trend in prominent dividends: ~60% in 1963 to ~30% in 2018. Decline coincides with the rise in institutional ownership. Time-series regressions (Table 13): a one-standard-deviation increase in institutional ownership is associated with a significant decline in dividend prominence, surviving controls for P/E ratio and market volatility.

## 8. EPS Clustering vs. Dividend Clustering (Table 11)

EPS shows only a slight tendency for increments of 5 (20.6% vs. 19.8% one penny below). No association between EPS clustering and dividend prominence in the two-way sort, suggesting the two phenomena have distinct drivers.
