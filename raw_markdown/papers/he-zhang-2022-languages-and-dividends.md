# Languages and dividends

- **Authors:** He, Wen; Zhang, Feida
- **Journal:** The British Accounting Review
- **Year:** 2022
- **DOI:** 10.1016/j.bar.2022.101132

## Abstract

We study whether languages are related to corporate dividend policies around the world. Users of languages with a weak future time reference (FTR), such as Japanese and Finnish, do not need to grammatically distinguish future and current events, while users of strong-FTR languages such as French and Italian do. Chen (2013) shows that people who use weak-FTR languages may perceive the future to be nearer and have less precise perceptions of the timing of future events than users of strong-FTR languages. We argue that these perceptions may result in a lower discount rate and a higher valuation of future dividends, leading to a weaker preference and demand for a dividend today. Using a large sample of firms from 19 markets, we find supporting evidence that firms in weak-FTR language markets pay lower dividends than firms in strong-FTR language markets. The results remain robust after a battery of robustness tests, including using a single market with multiple languages and using a difference-in-differences approach in a market with a change of official languages. Further evidence shows that weak-FTR languages are related to a lower implied cost of equity capital and stronger market reactions to dividend changes. Our results offer a new explanation for cross-country differences in dividend policies and add to the research on culture and financial markets.

## 1 Introduction

Miller and Modigliani (1961) propose that, in a frictionless market with rational investors, investors and firms should be indifferent to whether firms pay a one-dollar dividend today or retain the one dollar, invest it and then pay a higher dividend in the future. The rationale is that after proper discounting, the present value of the higher dividend in the future should be equal to one dollar. Later research, however, shows that firms make a deliberate effort to set their dividend policies and that investors have heterogeneous demands for dividends. The extant literature has developed various theories to explain the variations in corporate dividend policies by relaxing the assumptions underlying the proposition of Miller and Modigliani (1961), such as taxes, information frictions and agency problems. However, the literature seems to neglect a key factor in the proposition, namely the discount rate that investors use to calculate the present value of future dividends.

Linguistics studies document that some languages have a strong future time preference (FTR) and require speakers to mark future events grammatically. For example, English speakers use future tense and words such as "will" to refer to a future event. In contrast, languages with weak FTR, such as Chinese, Japanese, and German, have no such requirements. Because languages may influence speakers' representation of reality and decision making, the time preference in languages may affect people's perceptions of time. Specifically, Chen (2013) hypothesizes that users of weak-FTR languages may perceive time differently from speakers of strong-FTR languages in two ways. First, because weak-FTR languages do not distinguish the present and the future grammatically, speakers of weak-FTR languages may perceive a future event to be more imminent. Second, speakers of weak-FTR languages often have a more ambiguous perception of future timing. These differences in time perception may affect the decision making of individuals and firms.

Following these studies, we argue that languages and the associated differences in time perceptions may affect firms' and investors' calculation of the present value of and preferences for future dividends. If the present value of the expected dividend is higher than one dollar, the firm (and its investors) will prefer to retain the cash and delay the dividend. Holding E(D) constant, the differences in time perception associated with languages could affect the calculation of the present value of the expected dividend in two ways. First, a shorter time period perceived by weak-FTR language speakers could imply that the expected dividend is discounted for a shorter period of time, leading to a smaller t and a higher present value. Second, speakers of weak-FTR languages may perceive the timing of a future dividend with more ambiguity, while speakers of strong-FTR languages perceive the timing with more certainty. Because the present value is a convex function of time, Jensen's inequality implies that the ambiguity in timing may lead speakers of weak-FTR languages to have a higher expected present value of a future dividend. Therefore, both effects will result in speakers of weak-FTR languages having a high expected present value of a future dividend and thus preferring to delay the dividend to the future.

To test the effect of languages on dividends, we collect data on a large sample of firm-year observations from 19 markets around the world, including nine markets with weak-FTR languages. The final sample consists of 220,464 firm-year observations from 23,873 unique firms in 19 different markets.

In the multivariate analysis where we control for a number of firm characteristics, we find that weak-FTR languages are associated with lower dividends. To establish the causal effect of languages on dividends, we examine Hong Kong whose sovereignty was handed over from the UK to China in 1997. Before the handover, English was the official language in Hong Kong for governmental written communication and records. While the legal system and other institutional factors have remained similar in Hong Kong since 1997, the use of Chinese has gained popularity as Chinese has been emphasized as an official language. We find that firms in Hong Kong pay lower dividends after 1997 relative to benchmark firms.

We also focus on Switzerland, which has four official languages: French, German, Italian and Romansh. While French, Italian and Romansh are strong-FTR languages, German is classified as a weak-FTR language. We find that firms located in regions of Switzerland where German is spoken as the dominant language pay lower dividends than firms in other regions of Switzerland.

Finally, we find that the average implied cost of capital is negatively and significantly related to weak-FTR languages, after controlling for a number of firm and country characteristics. The cost of equity capital of firms in countries with weak-FTR languages is about 90 basis points lower than that of firms in markets with strong-FTR languages.
